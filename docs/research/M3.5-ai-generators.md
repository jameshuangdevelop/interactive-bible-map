# M3.5-07 — AI image generators for reconstructions: terms, license and recommendation

Fact-Checker & Licensing · Step 1 (research) of [M3.5-07](../tasks/M3.5-07-ai-reconstructions.md) · All sources read on 2026-09-30 (US Pacific time) · This is a reading of published terms, not legal advice.

ADR-0029 §7 has the human generate AI reconstructions from cited prompts. The project then hosts them as WebP files labelled "AI-generated reconstruction" and releases them for anyone to reuse. This report compares the generators the human could use and recommends a license, a credit line and a tool. Once the human has chosen, the rest of step 1 records the decision in `docs/LICENSES.md` and `ATTRIBUTION.md`, using the text proposed in §11, and finishes the schema's AI fields.

Sources are cited as [S*n*] and listed at the end with their URLs and versions. Project documents are cited by path.

## Summary

- **License for our AI images: CC0 1.0**, which confirms the card's default. In the United States, purely AI-generated images get no copyright, and prompts alone don't make the user an author [S34][S37]. Some other countries may grant a right [S34]. CC0 gives up whatever right exists, everywhere, and falls back to a permissive license where a waiver isn't possible [S38]. Creative Commons recommends CC0 for this kind of work [S40], and advises against putting CC licenses on public-domain works [S39]. See §7.
- **Recommended tool, ranked** (see §9):
  1. **OpenAI** (ChatGPT image generation, or the Images API if you want the agents to script it). It has the simplest terms: OpenAI assigns its rights in the output to you [S1][S2]. It asks for a clear AI disclosure [S4]. It acknowledges that file conversion can strip its provenance metadata [S5]. It also leads a public preference leaderboard, which is a non-authoritative signal [S46].
  2. **Google** (Nano Banana Pro in the Gemini app on a Google AI plan, or the Gemini API). Google doesn't claim ownership of outputs [S9][S12], so CC0 is fine. Two things need care: the visible "sparkle" watermark on Free and Google AI Pro images [S19], and the API's rule against use in a service that people under 18 are likely to use [S12].
  3. **Midjourney, on the Pro or Mega plan only.** Only those plans keep drafts private (Stealth Mode) [S24]. Pro or Mega is also needed to own the images if you work for a company with more than US$1M a year in revenue [S22]. Midjourney forbids automation [S23], and its own guide says long, detailed prompts "can confuse the process" [S25].
  - **Avoid Adobe Firefly.** Its terms forbid removing Content Credentials [S27][S28], which our WebP conversion can drop [S5]. They also forbid letting third parties use outputs for AI training [S27], and any open license would let them do that.
  - **Avoid FLUX.1 [dev]**, whose license is for non-commercial use only [S32]. **FLUX.1 [schnell]** is legally clean (Apache 2.0) [S31], but it needs a capable GPU or a hosting service whose own terms would then apply.
- **Credit line:** `AI-generated reconstruction · Interactive Bible Map, made with <Tool> (<model>), <YYYY-MM-DD> · CC0 1.0 · Based on: <sources>` (§8).
- **Decisions for the human:** the tool and plan, whether to generate by hand or by API, and confirmation of CC0. The questions are in §10.

## 1. OpenAI: ChatGPT image generation and the Images API

**(a) Ownership.** Under the ChatGPT Terms of Use (effective 2026-01-01), "as between you and OpenAI", you own the Output, and OpenAI assigns you "all our right, title, and interest, if any" in it [S1]. The Services Agreement, which covers API customers, says the same in §4.1 [S2]. Both warn that output "may not be unique": other users may receive similar images, and the assignment doesn't cover theirs [S1][S2].

**(b) Publishing and reuse by others.** Neither document limits where Output may be published or who may reuse it. The restrictions on Output bind the user. For example, you may not use Output "to develop models that compete with OpenAI" [S1]. API customers also must not let "End Users" do so [S2 §3.3]. End Users are people who use the customer's account or a "Customer Application", meaning an app that integrates with an OpenAI API [S2]. Our map doesn't integrate one, so its readers are not End Users. Releasing the images under CC0 is therefore consistent with these terms.

**(c) Disclosure and provenance.** The Terms of Use make the Sharing & Publication Policy binding [S1], and so does the Services Agreement [S2]. That policy, last updated in 2022, asks you to:
- review each generation before sharing it;
- attribute the content to "your name or your company";
- indicate that it is AI-generated "in a way no user could reasonably miss" [S4].

The Terms of Use also forbid representing Output as human-generated [S1].

Supported images from ChatGPT, Codex and the API carry both C2PA metadata and an invisible SynthID watermark [S5]. OpenAI notes that C2PA metadata "can sometimes be removed by ... file conversions", and that SynthID may survive such changes [S5]. A visible watermark appears only if you ask for one [S5]. No clause in [S1]–[S5] forbids removing the metadata. Our on-image label and the project credit meet the policy's disclosure and attribution requests.

**(d) Usage-policy limits.** The Usage Policies (effective 2025-10-29) forbid, among other things, violence and terrorism, using someone's likeness without consent, and deceit [S3]. Nothing in them restricts historical or religious subjects as such [S3]. Prompts that show places, with no people in the foreground (card M3.5-02), stay well clear of these limits.

**(e) Plan and cost.**
- **ChatGPT** lists Free ("Limited image creation"), Go, Plus and Pro ("Unlimited and faster image creation"). "Image generation with Thinking" is in Plus and Pro only [S6]. The amounts weren't in the page text.
- **API:** GPT Image 2.5 (Sunburst and Flare) costs US$8 per million image input tokens and US$30 per million image output tokens, or half that in Batch [S7][S8]. The cost of one image depends on its size and quality [S8].
- **Sizes:** the API offers 1536×1024 landscape and custom sizes up to 3,840 px on a side. Sizes above 2560×1440 are "experimental" [S8].

**(f) Prompt adherence (non-authoritative).** OpenAI's own guide lists three limits: text rendering, consistency across images, and "Composition Control" in "layout-sensitive compositions" [S8]. On the Text-to-Image Arena's overall leaderboard of 2026-09-24, GPT Image 2.5 Sunburst ranked 1st (score 1424, marked preliminary), Flare 2nd and GPT Image 2 3rd [S46]. The Arena ranks voters' preferences across all kinds of prompts, so it says little about architectural accuracy.

## 2. Google: Gemini app image generation and the Gemini API / Vertex AI

**Imagen is no longer an option in the Gemini API.** Google's Imagen page there says "Imagen models are shut down" and points to Nano Banana instead [S17]. Imagen on Vertex AI was not checked. The current image models are Nano Banana 2 (`gemini-3.1-flash-image`) and Nano Banana Pro (`gemini-3-pro-image`) [S14][S16]. Google's older Generative AI Additional Terms no longer apply, except to partners whose signed agreements reference them [S11].

**(a) Ownership.**
- **Gemini app:** the Google Terms of Service (effective 2026-07-30) say "Google won't claim ownership" of content you generate [S9].
- **Gemini API** (Additional Terms effective 2026-03-23): the same, and Google may generate "the same or similar content for others" [S12].
- **Vertex AI**, which Google's terms now call Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform: Generated Output counts as Customer Data, and Google "does not assert any ownership rights" in it [S13 §20(a)].

Google assigns nothing, but it has no claim that would need clearing.

**(b) Publishing and reuse by others.**
- **Publishing is expected.** The Gemini app help page tells users to use discretion before they "publish" generated content [S14].
- **Training limits bind the user only.** The Terms of Service forbid using AI-generated content from Google's services "to develop machine learning models" [S9]. These restrictions bind the user, so CC0 reuse by others is consistent with them.
- **The Gemini API is meant for professional use.** Google AI Studio and the Gemini API are "for professional or business purposes, not for consumer use" [S12]. Whether a free public study tool counts as professional use is a judgment call; the Gemini app has no such condition [S14].
- **Under-18 rule.** Gemini API users must be 18 or over, and must not use the Services in a website "likely to be accessed by individuals under the age of 18" [S12]. Vertex AI has the same rule [S13 §20(d)]. A Bible map will be used by young readers, so this is acceptable only as long as the app never calls the generator and only serves the finished files. The Gemini app needs users to be 13 or over, and 18 or over for editing [S14].
- **The free tier uses your prompts.** On AI Studio's free tier, Google uses what you submit to improve its products, and human reviewers may read it [S12]. Our prompts are public in the repository anyway.

**(c) Disclosure and provenance.**
- Every image from the Gemini API carries a SynthID watermark [S18]. SynthID is designed to withstand cropping, filters and lossy compression [S21].
- Since an announcement of 2025-11-20, Nano Banana Pro images in the Gemini app, Vertex AI and Google Ads also carry C2PA metadata [S20].
- The same day, Google said it would keep a **visible watermark (the Gemini sparkle)** on images made by Free and Google AI Pro users, and remove it for Google AI Ultra subscribers and in Google AI Studio [S19].

No clause found in [S9], [S10], [S12] or [S13] forbids removing C2PA metadata. The Terms of Service do say "Don't remove, obscure, or alter any of our branding, logos", but the surrounding text is about Google's own content [S9]. Whether that covers the sparkle on an image you generated is unclear, so we advise against cropping or retouching it out. Instead, generate where no visible mark is added: Google AI Ultra, AI Studio or the API.

On disclosure, Google forbids misrepresenting an image's provenance "by claiming it was created solely by a human" [S10], and "misleading others into thinking that generative AI content was created by a human" [S9].

**(d) Usage-policy limits.** The Generative AI Prohibited Use Policy (2024-12-17) bans hate speech, violence or incitement, sexually explicit content and deception, among other things [S10]. Google "may make exceptions" for "educational, documentary, scientific, or artistic considerations" [S10]. It has no rule about religious subjects as such [S10].

**(e) Plan and cost.**
- **Google AI plans:** US$0 (free), Google AI Plus US$4.99 a month, Google AI Pro US$19.99 a month, and Google AI Ultra US$99.99 a month, or US$199.99 for higher limits [S15].
- **Gemini app downloads** are 2K with a Google AI plan and 1K without one [S14]. 1K is below the 1,600 px our files may use (card step 3), so a plan is needed. Nano Banana Pro is fully available with a Google AI plan; free accounts get only limited access [S14][S15][S19].
- **Gemini API (paid tier),** per image [S16]:
  - Nano Banana 2: US$0.067 at 1K, US$0.101 at 2K and US$0.151 at 4K.
  - Nano Banana Pro: US$0.134 at 1K–2K and US$0.24 at 4K.

  For example, 300 Nano Banana Pro images at 2K would cost about US$40. That is our own arithmetic, allowing up to 3 images for each of the 31 places plus regenerations.

**(f) Prompt adherence (non-authoritative).** Google describes Nano Banana 2 as having "Improved world knowledge" and "Enhanced instruction following", and Nano Banana Pro as having "the highest level of world knowledge" [S14]. The API supports 16:9 and 21:9 landscape ratios [S18]. On the Arena leaderboard, Nano Banana 2 (with web search) scored 1261 and Nano Banana Pro (2K) 1247 [S46].

## 3. Midjourney (read from Internet Archive snapshots; see "How this was checked")

**(a) Ownership.** "You own all Assets You create ... to the fullest extent possible under applicable law", subject to the Terms and to third parties' rights [S22 §4]. One condition matters here. If you are "a company or any employee of a company" with more than US$1,000,000 a year in revenue, you must be on the Pro or Mega plan to own your Assets [S22]. The brief describes the human as a "professional software engineer" (`AGENT_TEAM_PROMPT.md`). If the human's employer is above that size, the clause's wording applies even to a personal project, so only Pro or Mega would be safe. Ownership continues after a downgrade or cancellation [S22]. All plans come with "General Commercial Terms" [S24].

Midjourney receives a perpetual, irrevocable license to reproduce, distribute and sublicense both your prompts and your images [S22].

**(b) Publishing and reuse by others.** Sharing outside Midjourney is allowed: "It's OK to share Your creations outside of the Midjourney community" [S22 §9]. However, "By default, Your Content is publicly viewable and remixable" [S22]. Stealth Mode, which keeps images private, exists only on Pro and Mega [S24], and Midjourney promises only "best efforts" under it [S22]. On Basic or Standard, drafts that the Fact-Checker rejects in step 4 would stay public on Midjourney's site. Nothing in the terms conflicts with CC0.

**(c) Disclosure and provenance.** No watermark or provenance rule was found in the Terms or the Community Guidelines [S22][S23]. Users "may not intentionally mislead recipients of the Assets about their nature or source" [S22].

**(d) Usage-policy limits.** Midjourney aims to be "PG-13 and family friendly" [S22]. It bans gore, which it defines to include "blood, violence" [S23], and content "derogatory to a community" [S23]. A crucifixion scene would run into the gore rule. Establishing shots of places should not.

**(e) Plan and cost.** Basic US$10, Standard US$30, Pro US$60 and Mega US$120 a month, or US$96, US$288, US$576 and US$1,152 a year [S24]. Midjourney has no public API, and automating it is "strictly prohibited" [S23]. Each account is for one user [S22]. Generation would therefore be by hand only.

**(f) Prompt adherence (non-authoritative).** Midjourney's own guide says "Short and simple prompts typically generate the best images" and that long lists or detailed instructions "can confuse the process" [S25]. Our prompts come from detailed research briefs. Midjourney does not appear in the Arena leaderboard's model list as we read it (78 of its 80 entries were in the page text) [S46].

## 4. Adobe Firefly

**(a) Ownership.** Under the General Terms (effective 2025-10-03), you keep ownership of your content, and Adobe claims none [S26 §4.2]. The Generative AI Product Specific Terms (effective 2026-04-23) make Input and Output "your Content" [S27 §1], and warn that Output "may not be protectable" [S27 §3.2].

**(b) Publishing and reuse by others: a conflict.** Firefly allows commercial use. The FAQ says outputs of non-beta features may be used in "commercial projects" [S29], and the General Terms allow generating content "for any other legal purpose" [S26 §17]. However, §3.3 of the Product Specific Terms says: "You must not, and must not allow third parties to, use ... any Outputs" to create, train or improve AI systems [S27].
- CC0 is an unconditional waiver [S38], so a CC0 release would let anyone train on the images.
- CC BY-SA 4.0 would also allow it.

We take the cautious reading that publishing under either license would "allow" third parties to train on the images. We see no open license that avoids this conflict.

Firefly also offers partner models "such as Google, OpenAI, Flux, and more" [S30]. Their use falls under the third parties' own terms (General Terms §3.12) and needs users to be 18 or over [S27 §7]. Adobe's §3 duties appear to apply to their outputs as well.

**(c) Disclosure and provenance: a conflict.** "You must not remove or alter any watermarks or Content Authenticity Initiative metadata (e.g., Content Credentials)" [S27 §3.1]. The User Guidelines repeat: "You must not remove, alter, or disable any Content Credentials" [S28 §6]. A plain conversion to WebP can drop this metadata [S5]. Keeping it would need a separate workflow that writes a new, signed C2PA record into the WebP. That is extra tooling the project doesn't have.

**(d) Usage-policy limits.** The Guidelines ban content that "attacks or dehumanizes a group based on ... religion", content that glorifies graphic violence or gore, and misleading content. They also ban "unauthorized automated or scripting processes" [S28].

**(e) Plan and cost.** Firefly Standard comes with 2,000 generative credits, Pro with 4,000, Pro Plus with 10,000 and Premium with 50,000. All paid plans include "unlimited standard image" generations, and partner models use credits [S30]. The amounts weren't in the page text. Adobe's indemnity for outputs applies only to eligible team and enterprise plans, and is capped at US$10,000 per output or claim [S27 §8].

**(f) Prompt adherence (non-authoritative).** Adobe says Firefly's models are trained on licensed content and public-domain content [S29]. No Firefly model appears in the Arena leaderboard's model list as we read it [S46].

## 5. Open weights: FLUX.1 [schnell] (and why not FLUX.1 [dev])

**FLUX.1 [schnell]**
- **(a)(b) License.** Released under Apache 2.0, it "can be used for personal, scientific, and commercial purposes" [S31]. Apache 2.0 grants a "perpetual, worldwide" and "royalty-free" license to the model itself [S33]. Neither the model card nor the license says anything about who owns outputs.
- **(c) Provenance.** The model card states no provenance requirement [S31].
- **(d) Use limits.** Out-of-scope uses include generating personal information that could harm someone [S31].
- **(e) Cost.** The weights are free. The model has 12 billion parameters [S31], so it needs a capable GPU. The machine used for the M3-12 benchmark had "no GPU acceleration" (`docs/research/M3-12-map-benchmark.md`). A hosting service would add its own terms, which weren't reviewed here.
- **(f) Prompt adherence (non-authoritative).** The model card claims "competitive prompt following" [S31]. The Arena's top 30 includes only FLUX.2 models, which are proprietary or under a non-commercial license [S46].

**FLUX.1 [dev]** is licensed only for "non-commercial and non-production use" [S32]. One clause allows commercial use of outputs, but another forbids using "any data produced by" the model for commercial purposes [S32]. The license contradicts itself on this point, and our CC0 release allows commercial reuse, so avoid it.

## 6. Copyright in purely AI-generated images

**United States.** The US Copyright Office's *Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2: Copyrightability* was published on 2025-01-29 [S35]. Its conclusions [S34]:
- Copyright "does not extend to purely AI-generated material, or material where there is insufficient human control over the expressive elements".
- With current tools, "prompts do not alone provide sufficient control". The report also says prompts "convey unprotectible ideas".
- Human-authored expressive inputs that can be perceived in an output, and creative selection, arrangement or modification of outputs, can be protected.

The Office's 2023 registration guidance requires applicants to disclose AI-generated content [S36]. In *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, decided on 2025-03-18, the D.C. Circuit upheld the refusal to register an AI-generated image. It held that the Copyright Act requires a work "to be authored in the first instance by a human being" [S37]. Later appeals were not checked.

**Elsewhere** (from the same report's survey) [S34 §III]:
- **EU:** most member states said AI output can be protected "only if the human input ... was significant".
- **UK:** the law protects computer-generated works that have no human author, and names as author the person who made "the arrangements necessary" to create them.
- **China:** the Beijing Internet Court (2023) held an AI image protected because of more than 150 prompts and later adjustments.

**What this means for our images (our interpretation).** Our images will come from text prompts, and we will only resize them and convert them to WebP. These are technical changes, not the "creative modifications" the report describes. So the images are most likely not protected by copyright in the US. In the UK and some other countries a right may exist, and it would probably belong to the person who made the arrangements: the human.

The report's point about expressive inputs has a practical consequence. If someone else's image were uploaded as a reference, its expression could carry into the output, along with its license terms [S34]. Prompts should therefore be text only (§10).

**Disclosure law beyond the vendors: the EU AI Act.** From 2 August 2026 [S43][S44], Article 50(4) of the EU AI Act requires "deployers" to disclose AI-generated images that constitute a "deep fake". For evidently artistic, creative or similar works, the duty is limited to disclosing the AI content "in an appropriate manner" [S41].
- A **deep fake** is AI imagery that "resembles existing persons, objects, places" and "would falsely appear to a person to be authentic or truthful" [S42].
- A **deployer** excludes use "in the course of a personal non-professional activity" [S42].
- **Machine-readable marking** of outputs is the provider's duty, not ours [S41 §2].

We have not assessed whether the Act applies to this project. Our visible label would meet Article 50(4) either way.

## 7. Recommended license: CC0 1.0

**Recommendation:** release every published AI reconstruction under **CC0 1.0 Universal**. The reasons:
1. **It matches the law in both cases.** Where no copyright exists (the US, per §6), CC0 states that plainly. Where some right does exist (for example in the UK), CC0 waives it "in all territories worldwide", with a fallback license if the waiver fails [S38].
2. **It is what Creative Commons recommends.** CC encourages "the use of CC0 for those works that do not involve a significant degree of human creativity" [S40]. It also says CC licenses "should not be applied to works in the worldwide public domain" [S39].
3. **It fits the project's licenses.** CC0 is already one of the accepted image licenses (ADR-0012; `docs/LICENSES.md`), and `docs/LICENSES.md` already treats CC0 content as compatible with the CC BY-SA 4.0 data.
4. **It fits the recommended tools' terms.** OpenAI, Google and Midjourney don't restrict how others reuse outputs (§§1–3).

**Alternatives considered:**
- **CC BY-SA 4.0** (the content license): rejected, because it would claim rights that likely don't exist in the US [S39], and might make reusers think they must share alike.
- **CC BY 4.0:** rejected for the same reason.
- **Public Domain Mark:** rejected. It is meant for works already in the public domain worldwide [S39], and ours may not be in the UK.

**The prompts stay CC BY-SA 4.0.** The briefs and prompts in `content/` keep the project's content license. The project is their licensor, and ShareAlike binds reusers, not the licensor. The Copyright Office also treats prompts as conveying ideas [S34]. So the project can release images made from them under CC0.

**What CC0 does not change:**
- **Our own duties to the vendor.** For example, the human must not use the outputs to train competing models [S1].
- **The label.** CC0 can't make reusers keep the "AI-generated reconstruction" label, so the credit can only ask them to. If the PO wants the disclosure to travel with the file, two options exist:
  - a small caption burned into a corner of the image;
  - an embedded XMP tag with IPTC's `trainedAlgorithmicMedia` ("Created using Generative AI") [S45].

  Neither is required by any terms read here.

## 8. Credit line and data fields

**On the image:** the label **"AI-generated reconstruction"** (ADR-0029 §7.6).

**Credit line in the panel** (the card's format, with the project named as author):

```text
AI-generated reconstruction · Interactive Bible Map, made with <Tool> (<model>), <YYYY-MM-DD> · CC0 1.0 · Based on: <source title>; <source title>; …
```

For example: `AI-generated reconstruction · Interactive Bible Map, made with ChatGPT (<model shown in ChatGPT>), 2026-10-05 · CC0 1.0 · Based on: <brief source 1>; <brief source 2>`.
- "CC0 1.0" links to <https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/>.
- Each "Based on" item links to the place's Sources list.
- Naming the project meets OpenAI's request to attribute content to "your name or your company" [S4]. It also tells readers who checked the image.
- The date is the generation date the human records in step 2.

## 10. Rules for steps 2 and 3, whichever tool is chosen

1. **Text prompts only.** Don't upload reference images unless they are public domain or CC0, and record any that are used [S34].
2. **No real people's likenesses,** and no people in the foreground (card M3.5-02) [S3][S23].
3. **Keep the original PNGs,** with their embedded metadata, in `media/ai-incoming/` (ignored by Git). Back them up outside the repository for as long as the images are published.
4. **Converting to WebP may drop C2PA metadata** for OpenAI, Google, Midjourney and FLUX images (§§1–3, 5), **but not for Firefly** [S27]. SynthID, where present, stays in the pixels [S5][S21].
5. **Never crop, blur or paint out a visible watermark** (§2c).
6. **Never call a generator from the app.** The site serves finished files only [S12][S13].
7. **Record the tool, model and date** for every image (card step 2).
8. **Midjourney only:** turn on Stealth before the first generation [S24].

**Questions for the human:**
1. Which tool and plan?
2. Will you generate by hand, or give the agents an API key? Midjourney and Firefly don't allow scripting [S23][S28].
3. Do you confirm CC0 1.0?
4. Should the label also be burned into the image or embedded as metadata (§7)?
