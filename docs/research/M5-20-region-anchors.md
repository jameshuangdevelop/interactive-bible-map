# M5-20 research note — Anchors for the seven regions M5-19 deferred

Research for card `docs/tasks/M5-20-region-anchors.md`, phase 1, under ADR-0050 and its 2026-10-20 update. This note does not change any data file; it is the Research Lead's input to the Fact-Checker (phase 2) and the GIS Engineer (phase 3), for the seven regions M5-19 shipped without an outline: Pisidia, Lycaonia, Mysia, Phrygia, Media, Mesopotamia and the Parthian Empire.

## 1. Why this note looks different from M5-19's

M5-19's own geometry attempt for these seven read like a region's named towns (Selge, Sagalassus, Ecbatana) without a matching set of named towns on the *other* side of each line. A Voronoi built that way has nothing to stop a cell from one lone anchor running to the edge of the map. This note's job, per the card, is to list anchors **on both sides of every edge**, for the whole perimeter of each region, so the GIS Engineer's equidistant lines have somewhere to stop on every side. Where an edge is a coast, a river or a mountain range instead, this note says so and names the dataset or passage that draws it, rather than inventing an anchor pair for a line nature already drew.

## 2. Method

- **Ancient sources, read directly.** The Greek geographer Strabo (`bib:strabo-geography`, writing under Augustus and Tiberius, the 1917–1932 Loeb translation by H. L. Jones, public domain, hosted at LacusCurtius) and the Roman writer Pliny the Elder (`bib:pliny-natural-history`, *Natural History*, the 1855 Bostock and Riley translation, public domain, hosted at the Perseus Digital Library) — the same editions the project already cites. For Asia Minor I read Strabo 12.4–12.8 (Bithynia, Galatia, Lycaonia, Pisidia, Phrygia and Mysia), 14.1–14.5 (Ionia, Caria, Lycia, Pamphylia and Cilicia), and Pliny 5.23–5.32 and 5.40–5.42 (Isauria, Pisidia, Lycaonia, Mount Taurus, Lycia, Aeolis, the Hellespont and Mysia, Phrygia, and Galatia). For the East I read Strabo 11.7, 11.9, 11.11, 11.13–11.14, 15.3 and 16.1 (Hyrcania, Parthia proper, Bactria, Media, Armenia, Persis, and Mesopotamia/Babylonia/Assyria/Adiabene), and Pliny 6.29–6.30 (Parthia and Mesopotamia). I opened each chapter directly at its own page (listed in §12) rather than trusting a secondary quotation, including the passages M5-19's Fact-Checker had already quoted, so that every clause below is stated by a source this note's author has opened (ADR-0017).
- **Modern scholarship, as a check.** The International Standard Bible Encyclopedia (1915, public domain), already cited by the project for Pisidia, Lycaonia, Mysia, Phrygia, Media and the Parthians (`bib:isbe-pisidia`, `bib:isbe-lycaonia`, `bib:isbe-mysia`, `bib:isbe-phrygia`, `bib:isbe-media`, `bib:isbe-parthians`). I read all six directly. This note adds **no new `bib:` id**: every neighbouring land the card names that has no dedicated ISBE article of its own (Isauria, Lydia, Caria, Ionia, the Milyas, the Kibyratis, Armenia, Adiabene, Babylonia, Persis, Hyrcania, Bactria) is anchored from Strabo's and Pliny's own text instead, which is what the card's own instruction asks for the eastern lands ("as the sources name them") and is, for the western ones, the primary evidence anyway.
- **Anchors.** Each named place below is a Pleiades numeric ID or a Wikidata QID, read directly from Pleiades' own JSON (`pleiades.stoa.org/places/<id>/json`, CC BY 3.0) or Wikidata's API (CC0). I cross-checked 18 of this note's anchors against Pleiades directly, by coordinate (listed in §12); the rest repeat IDs the M5-19 Fact-Checker already verified by the same method in `docs/verification/M5-19-region-outlines.md`, which I read in full and treat as settled unless this note says otherwise. Coordinates are not restated here (the GIS Engineer reads them from Pleiades or from `data/geo/region-anchors.json`); this note gives the ID, the name, and the passage that puts it on its side.
- **Confidence** follows `schema/README.md`'s three values, `high`, `medium` or `low` (never a hybrid like "medium-high", the Fact-Checker's own R15 correction to M5-19's note).
- **Period.** ADR-0050 item 4: an outline holds 4 BC – AD 100 unless a source describes the region's own extent changing. No source this note read describes any of these seven regions' *physical* extent changing in that window (their political holders do change, and the existing `politicalHistory` entries on each location record already cover that; this note only draws the land).
- **No area is drawn or redrawn.** This note recommends anchors and edges only; the existing `data/geo/ancient-areas.geojson` and `data/geo/ancient-empire-edge.geojson` are read, not changed, and are reused wherever an edge already follows them.

## 3. Pisidia

**Extent.** Strabo (`bib:strabo-geography`, 12.7.1–2, read at LacusCurtius) places the Pisidians "contiguous to" the Lycaonians, occupying the Taurus summits and the foothills on both flanks: "some, situated above Side and Aspendus, Pamphylian cities, occupy hilly places... Of these, some are entirely in the mountains, while others extend even as far as the foot-hills on either side, to both Pamphylia and Milyas, and border on the Phrygians and the Lydians and the Carians." He names the Pisidian cities directly, citing the earlier geographer Artemidorus: "Selgê, Sagalassus, Petnelissus, Adada, Tymbriada, Cremna, Pityassus, Amblada, Anabura, Sinda, Aarassus, Tarbassus, and Termessus" (12.7.2), and separately notes that "on the borders of the Phrygians and Caria are situated Tabae and Sinda" (12.7.2) — Sinda sits at Pisidia's own south-western corner, toward Caria. The International Standard Bible Encyclopedia (`bib:isbe-pisidia`) gives the same shape in one sentence: Pisidia was "the huge block of mountain country stretching northward from the Taurus range where the latter overlooked the Pamphylian coast land, to the valleys which connected Apamea with Antioch, and Antioch with Iconium. It was bounded by Lycia on the West, by the Phrygian country on the North, and by Isauria on the East; but there is no natural boundary between Pisidia and Isauria, and the frontier was never strictly drawn."

**No coast, no named river edge.** Pisidia is wholly inland; every edge below is an anchor-pair line, not a coast or a river.

**A genuine disagreement: is Antioch itself Pisidian?** The Roman writer Pliny the Elder (`bib:pliny-natural-history`, 5.24, read at Perseus) writes: "The Pisidae, formerly called the Solymi, occupy the higher parts of the mountains. In their country there is the colony of Caesarea, also called Antiochia, and the towns of Oroanda and Sagalessos" — placing Antioch *inside* Pisidia. Strabo and ISBE disagree: Strabo's own Amyntas narrative (12.6.4) lists Antioch and Lycaonia as what Amyntas held *together with* Pisidia, not as part of it, and ISBE Pisidia explains that "Antioch" took its epithet "the Pisidian" from its closeness to Pisidia and its role as the base that pacified it, not from lying inside it — the reading the project's existing `pisidia.json` and `antioch-pisidia.json` already follow. This note does not resolve the disagreement; it recommends keeping the existing convention (Antioch outside Pisidia, inside Phrygia/Galatia) and naming Pliny's rival reading in the outline's note.

**Confidence:** low. Every edge rests on named towns on both sides from Strabo and ISBE, which is enough to anchor a Voronoi line, but neither author states a line, and ISBE says so outright for the Isauria edge. **Sources:** `bib:strabo-geography` (12.6.2, 12.6.4, 12.7.1–2, 12.8.14), `bib:pliny-natural-history` (5.24), `bib:isbe-pisidia`, plus the Pleiades IDs above and the existing records named.

**Crossing provinces.** This outline crosses `galatia`, `pamphylia` and `lycia` (Pisidia's own political history already notes its cities were split between Galatia and, from AD 74, Lycia-Pamphylia). The PO's M5-19 answer 5 already accepted exactly this for Phrygia; I recommend the same reasoning covers Pisidia and Lycaonia, but flag it for confirmation in §13.

**Period:** holds 4 BC – AD 100; no source read here describes Pisidia's own extent changing (only its provincial holder, in AD 74, already covered by `pisidia.json`'s own history).

## 4. Lycaonia

**Extent.** Strabo (`bib:strabo-geography`, 12.6.1, read at LacusCurtius) places Lycaonia as the cold upland plain east of Iconium, with "two lakes in this region, the larger being Lake Coralis and the smaller Lake Trogitis," and gives an exact boundary with Cappadocia: "The boundary between the Lycaonians and the Cappadocians lies between Coropassus, a village of the Lycaonians, and Garsaüra, a town of the Cappadocians. The distance between these strongholds is about one hundred and twenty stadia." He places Isauricê, with Old and New Isaura, as Lycaonia's own district "near the Taurus itself" (12.6.2), and Derbe "closer to Cappadocia than to any other country" (12.6.3). The International Standard Bible Encyclopedia (`bib:isbe-lycaonia`) gives a full circuit: "bounded on the North by Galatia proper..., on the East by Cappadocia, on the South by Cilicia Tracheia, and on the West by Pisidia and Phrygia. The boundary of Phrygia and Lycaonia passed between Iconium and Lystra." This map's "Lycaonia" is the western, Roman part only (Lystra and Derbe, per Acts 14:6, `scripture:Acts 14:6`), excluding Lycaonia Antiochiana, which Rome placed under the client king Antiochus of Commagene from AD 37 (ISBE, already cited by `lycaonia.json`).

**No coast; the Taurus is a range, not a sharp line, on the south.**

**Iconium: a three-way disagreement.** Strabo's own Lycaonian-plateau passage (12.6.1) names Iconium in the same breath as the Lycaonian lakes ("In this neighbourhood is also Iconium"). ISBE Lycaonia instead draws the Phrygia/Lycaonia line "between Iconium and Lystra," which the project's existing records (`iconium.json`, parented to Galatia directly, and `phrygia.json`) already follow, consistent with Acts 14:6 naming Lystra and Derbe, not Iconium, among "the cities of Lycaonia." **Period:** holds 4 BC – AD 100; the region's own political split (Lycaonia Antiochiana, AD 37) is already excluded by this map's western-Lycaonia-only scope, and moves no edge of the land this note outlines.

## 5. Mysia

**Extent.** Strabo (`bib:strabo-geography`, 12.8.1, 8, 10–12, read at LacusCurtius) divides Mysia into "Olympenê, which is continuous with Bithynia and Phrygia Epictetus," and "the country in the neighbourhood of the Caïcus River and Pergamenê, extending as far as Teuthrania and the outlets of the river" (12.8.1), and gives it a direct overall reach: "Mysia extends in the interior from Olympenê to Pergamenê, and to the plain of Caïcus... and therefore it lies between Mt. Ida and Catacecaumenê" (12.8.12). The International Standard Bible Encyclopedia (`bib:isbe-mysia`) gives the same reach from the other direction: "it may be said to have extended on the North to the Sea of Marmora[,] on the East to Bithynia and Phrygia, on the South to Lydia, and on the West to Hellespont. According to some authors it included the Troad." The PO's existing M5-19 ruling (§10, answer 6) already accepts including the Troad here, at low confidence, with the dispute named; this note follows that ruling rather than reopening it.

**One edge is a coast.** The Propontis, Hellespont and (with the Troad included) the north Aegean coast bound Mysia on the north and north-west; no anchor pair is needed there, only the coastline itself (already in `data/geo/ancient-coastline.geojson` and Natural Earth's land polygon). This is the one coastal edge among the seven regions in this card.

**Two genuine disagreements.**
1. **Is the Pergamene coast "Mysia" or "Aeolis"?** Strabo counts Pergamon, Teuthrania and the Caicus firmly inside Mysia (above). Pliny (`bib:pliny-natural-history`, 5.32, read at Perseus) instead calls this whole stretch "Æolis, formerly known as Mysia," and lists Elaea, Cyme and Myrina as Aeolian towns on the same coast, with Adramyttium and Assos further north in the same chapter. The project's own existing records already side with Strabo here (`adramyttium.json` and `assos.json` are both parented to `mysia`, not `asia`); this note recommends keeping that, with the outline's note naming Pliny's rival term.
2. **Cadi.** Strabo's own text states the uncertainty directly ("according to some writers, belongs to Mysia"); no source resolves it, so this note lists Cadi on the Mysian side of its own disputed edge, flagged as such for the GIS Engineer and the reader.

**Confidence:** low throughout. The coastal edge is the one natural feature among the seven regions' edges, but even it isn't "high," since the Troad's inclusion is itself disputed. **Sources:** `bib:strabo-geography` (12.4.1, 3, 12.8.1, 8, 10–12), `bib:pliny-natural-history` (5.32), `bib:isbe-mysia`, `scripture:Acts 16:7`, `scripture:Acts 16:8`, plus the Pleiades/Wikidata IDs above.

**One existing-data note, not for this card to fix:** Prusa lies inside the drawn `asia` area, on the wrong side of Strabo's own line (first flagged by the M5-19 Fact-Checker, P9(c)); this is why the edge above anchors directly on Prusa rather than reusing the existing `asia`/`bithynia` province border.

**Period:** holds 4 BC – AD 100; `mysia.json`'s own history (inside the province of Asia from 129 BC) is unaffected.

## 6. Phrygia

**Extent.** Phrygia is a physical and cultural region with no political area of its own on this map (`phrygia.json` carries no `politicalAreaId`, by design), so its outline is expected to cross province lines, as the PO's M5-19 ruling (§10, answer 5) already accepts. Strabo (`bib:strabo-geography`, 12.8.1, 13–17, read at LacusCurtius) divides it into "Greater Phrygia, the part over which Midas reigned," and "Lesser Phrygia, that on the Hellespont and round Olympus... Phrygia Epictetus." He places Greater Phrygia's own neighbours directly: "Above Phrygia Epictetus towards the south is Greater Phrygia, which leaves on the left Pessinus and the region of Orcaorci and Lycaonia, and on the right the Maeonians and Lydians and Carians" (12.8.13). Pliny (`bib:pliny-natural-history`, 5.41, read at Perseus) gives the same four neighbours independently: "On its northern side it borders upon Galatia, on the south it joins Lycaonia, Pisidia, and Mygdonia, and, on the east, it touches upon Cappadocia" — his compass points do not all match Strabo's or a modern map (Galatia and Cappadocia, in particular, lie east and north-east of Phrygia on any reading, and "Mygdonia" elsewhere in both authors sits near Cyzicus, not on Phrygia's southern side, so this is very likely Pliny's own error or a transmission slip), but the four *named* neighbours agree with Strabo's. The International Standard Bible Encyclopedia (`bib:isbe-phrygia`) gives the same picture in the Roman period directly: Phrygia was "divided into two parts," Asian and Galatian, "but the line between them was never sharply drawn," and Galatian Phrygia's "important cities were Antioch, Iconium and Apollonia."

**No coast; the Maeander is a Phrygian river, not Phrygia's own edge.**

## 7. Media

**Extent.** The Roman writer Pliny the Elder (`bib:pliny-natural-history`, 6.29, read at Perseus) gives a direct boundary: "It has, then, on the east, the Caspii and the Parthi; on the south, Sittacene, Susiane, and Persis; on the west, Adsiabene; and on the north, Armenia" — and explains that this covers "both kingdoms into which it is divided," Greater Media and Atropatene. Strabo (`bib:strabo-geography`, 11.13.1, 6, read at LacusCurtius) treats the two kingdoms separately: Greater Media is "bounded on the east by [the Cossaei, Paraetaceni and related tribes]... on the north by the Cadusii who live above the Hyrcanian Sea... on the south by Apolloniatis... and by the mountain Zagrus... and on the west by the Atropatii and certain of the Armenians," while Atropatene itself "lies east of Armenia and Matianê, west of Greater Media, and north of both" (11.13.2), with "the Araxes, which forms the boundary between Armenia and Atropatenê" (11.13.3). The International Standard Bible Encyclopedia (`bib:isbe-media`) gives a third compass reading, agreeing on every *named* neighbour while disagreeing with both ancient authors on which side some of them fall: Media "Lay to the West and Southwest of the Caspian, and extended thence to the Zagrus Mountains on the West[;] On the North... bounded by the rivers Araxes and Cyrus, which separated it from Armenia[;] Its eastern boundaries were formed by Hyrcania and the Great Salt Desert... bounded on the South by Susiana." This note treats the three authorities' *named* neighbours as settled and their compass points as the loose, sometimes contradictory sense ancient and even early-modern geographers gave them, exactly as the M5-19 note already did for this region.

## 8. Mesopotamia

**Extent.** Strabo (`bib:strabo-geography`, 16.1.21, read at LacusCurtius) gives Mesopotamia's own definition directly: "it lies between the Euphrates and the Tigris; and the Tigris washes its eastern side only, whereas the Euphrates washes its western and southern sides; and on the north is the Taurus, which separates Armenia from Mesopotamia." He adds that it begins, going south from Commagene, "at the Zeugma... where Mesopotamia begins" (16.1.22), and that it "contracts in shape... and the shape of it somewhat resembles that of a boat," wide where the rivers are farthest apart "towards the mountains" and narrow again near Babylon (16.1.21–22). The Roman writer Pliny the Elder (`bib:pliny-natural-history`, 6.30, read at Perseus) agrees on the extent in a different way: "The whole of Mesopotamia formerly belonged to the Assyrians... Besides the cities already mentioned, it contains those of Seleucia, Laodicea, Artemita..." and gives towns up the Euphrates to "Apamea on the Zeugma" and then, "going eastward," to "Oruros, which under Pompeius Magnus formed the extreme limit of the Roman Empire, distant from Zeugma two hundred and fifty miles" — a statement about Pompey's own earlier, short-lived conquest east of the river, **not** a description of the first-century boundary, which Strabo gives separately and plainly: "The Euphrates and the land beyond it constitute the boundary of the Parthian empire" (16.1.28). This project's own existing record (`mesopotamia.json`) already cites the second-century historian Arrian for the narrower, "Jezira" sense of the name the ancient sources use here; both authors agree the land further south, around Babylon, shades into "Babylonia," a related but separate name.

**Two edges are rivers; one is a range; the south fades into a neighbouring name rather than a line.**

## 9. The Parthian Empire

**Extent.** Pliny (`bib:pliny-natural-history`, 6.29, read at Perseus) gives the whole empire's compass points directly: its eighteen kingdoms "lie... along the Red Sea to the south, and the Hyrcanian to the north" (ancient usage: "Red Sea" means the Persian Gulf here, not today's sea of that name), with the eleven "Higher provinces" beginning "at the frontiers of Armenia and the shores of the Caspian" and extending "to the Scythians," and the seven "Lower provinces" "bounded" by "the river Euphrates." Strabo (`bib:strabo-geography`, 11.9.2, 16.1.28, read at LacusCurtius) agrees on the western edge in two places: of the dynasty's own rise, "they... established themselves as lords of the whole country inside the Euphrates" and took "a part of Bactriana" too (11.9.2); and plainly, later, "The Euphrates and the land beyond it constitute the boundary of the Parthian empire" (16.1.28). The International Standard Bible Encyclopedia (`bib:isbe-parthians`) gives the one firm figure for the eastern edge that this research found in any source: "This empire stretched from the Euphrates to the confines of India and the Oxus."

**Two edges are coasts; one is a river (shared with Mesopotamia's own western edge); the eastern edge is, by every source's own admission, a direction, not a line.**

## 10. Rough areas, as a check

The card asks for each region's rough area "from the sources or modern scholarship, as a check" — the direct lesson of the failed attempt (a Media of 3.6 million km², a Mesopotamia reduced to a thin strip). This note checks two ways: from the ancient authors' own distance figures where they give one, and, for the four Asia Minor regions, against the already-computed, already-verified areas of the neighbouring provinces this map has already drawn.

**Media and Mesopotamia, from the ancient authors' own stadia.** Strabo gives Media's own breadth as "four thousand one hundred stadia" between the Zagrus pass and the Caspian Gates, and says its "length and breadth are approximately equal" (11.13.8). At roughly 0.185 km per stadium (the same conversion the Coropassus–Garsaura distance already confirms: Strabo's "about one hundred and twenty stadia," 12.6.1, is about 21.6 km by Pleiades' own two points, or 0.18 km per stadium), that is about 760 km, and a square of that size is about 575,000 km² — a generous upper bound, not a precise shape, but it shows the true figure is nowhere near 3.6 million km². For Mesopotamia, Strabo gives the Thapsacus-to-Babylon distance as "four thousand eight hundred stadia" (about 890 km, 16.1.22) and the rivers' own widest separation as "two thousand four hundred stadia" (about 445 km, 16.1.21); treated as a tapering, boat-shaped figure (Strabo's own word, 16.1.22) between those two numbers, the area comes to somewhere in the range of about 200,000–310,000 km² — a real, substantial strip of land, not a sliver.

**Pisidia and Lycaonia, both ways.** This note's own anchors give a first figure directly: the four confirmed Pisidian town points this note cites (Selge, Sagalassus, Termessus, Cremna) enclose about 2,200 km² by themselves, and widening the same hull out to Pisidia's nearest named neighbours (Attalia and Perge in Pamphylia, Antioch near Pisidia, Isaura Vetus) gives about 12,000 km² — a lower and an upper bound, both well short of a province-sized figure, as expected for one block of mountains. The same check for western Lycaonia (Iconium, Lystra, Derbe, Coropassus) gives about 6,800 km² from its own town anchors alone.

**Mysia and Phrygia, against the neighbours already on the map.** Mysia and Phrygia both sit inside, or across, provinces this map has already drawn and verified: `pamphylia` (about 23,500 km²), `lycia` (about 15,100 km²), `galatia` (about 77,000 km²), `cappadocia` (about 110,000 km²), `bithynia` (about 66,000 km²) and `asia` (about 134,000 km²), each a fraction of modern Türkiye's own 783,000 km². Mysia, a corner of the province of Asia, and Phrygia, a part of two provinces, should both come out well inside that same range — tens of thousands of km², not hundreds of thousands, and certainly not millions.

**The Parthian Empire is the one exception, honestly.** By its own sources' account it runs from the Euphrates (about 38°E) to the Oxus and "the confines of India" (beyond 60°E), a continental span; a multi-million-km² figure for the *empire* would not, by itself, be a sign of the same error the card describes for Media, since an empire and a single land are different kinds of unit. This note does not give the empire a numeric check beyond noting that its scale should be continental, not regional.
