# M5-20 research note — Anchors for the seven deferred regions

> **Read the verification report first.** The Fact-Checker's report, `docs/verification/M5-20-region-anchors.md` (phase 2, `af50382`), checks the first version of this note and finds 49 corrections (R1–R49); the PO's rulings on its nine open items (PO-1 to PO-9, `9e5846a`) are appended at the report's end. This note applies all of them. Where this note and an earlier draft differ, this note and the report govern.

Research for card `docs/tasks/M5-20-region-anchors.md`, phase 1, under ADR-0050 and its 2026-10-20 update. This note does not change any data file; it is the Research Lead's input to the GIS Engineer (phase 3), for the seven regions M5-19 shipped without an outline: Pisidia, Lycaonia, Mysia, Phrygia, Media, Mesopotamia and the Parthian Empire. **Per PO-1, six of the seven are outlined; the Parthian Empire is not** (§9).

## 1. Why this note looks different from M5-19's

M5-19's own geometry attempt for these seven read like a region's named towns (Selge, Sagalassus, Ecbatana) without a matching set of named towns on the *other* side of each line. A Voronoi built that way has nothing to stop a cell from one lone anchor running to the edge of the map. This note's job, per the card, is to list anchors **on both sides of every edge**, for the whole perimeter of each region, so the GIS Engineer's equidistant lines have somewhere to stop on every side. Where an edge is a coast, a river or a mountain range instead, this note says so and names the dataset or passage that draws it, rather than inventing an anchor pair for a line nature already drew.

## 2. Method

- **Ancient sources, read directly.** The Greek geographer Strabo (`bib:strabo-geography`, writing under Augustus and Tiberius, the 1917–1932 Loeb translation by H. L. Jones, public domain, hosted at LacusCurtius) and the Roman writer Pliny the Elder (`bib:pliny-natural-history`, *Natural History*, the 1855 Bostock and Riley translation, public domain, hosted at the Perseus Digital Library) — the same editions the project already cites. Phase 1 read Strabo 11.7, 11.9, 11.11, 11.13–11.14, 12.4–12.8, 14.3–14.4, 15.3 and 16.1, and Pliny 5.24, 5.25, 5.32, 5.41, 6.29 and 6.30. This round reopened several of those same pages to re-verify a corrected reading, and added Strabo 13.4 (Pergamum, Sardis, Catacecaumenê, Hierapolis), which the verification report cites for several corrections (Sinda and the Cibyratis, Philadelphia, Carura and Antioch on the Maeander, and the Milyas). Every clause below is stated by a source this note's author has opened (ADR-0017), not copied from the report's own quotations.
- **Modern scholarship, as a check.** The International Standard Bible Encyclopedia (1915, public domain), already cited by the project for Pisidia, Lycaonia, Mysia, Phrygia, Media and the Parthians (`bib:isbe-pisidia`, `bib:isbe-lycaonia`, `bib:isbe-mysia`, `bib:isbe-phrygia`, `bib:isbe-media`, `bib:isbe-parthians`). This note adds **no new `bib:` id**.
- **Anchors.** Each named place below is a Pleiades numeric ID or a Wikidata QID, read directly from Pleiades' own JSON (`pleiades.stoa.org/places/<id>/json`, CC BY 3.0) or Wikidata's API (CC0). I cross-checked 21 of this note's anchors against Pleiades directly in phase 1; this round confirmed the ten new ids the Fact-Checker flagged as resting on Wikidata alone, plus Babylon's corrected id, Bactra, and one further anchor this round adds (Matiane), for 34 anchors spot-checked directly in total (§12). The rest repeat ids the M5-19 Fact-Checker verified.
- **Confidence** follows `schema/README.md`'s three values, `high`, `medium` or `low` — one value per region, never a hybrid.
- **Period.** ADR-0050 item 4: an outline holds 4 BC – AD 100 unless a source describes the region's own extent changing. No source read in either round describes any of these seven regions' *physical* extent changing in that window.
- **No area is drawn or redrawn.** This note recommends anchors and edges only.

## 3. Pisidia

**Extent.** Strabo (`bib:strabo-geography`, 12.7.1–2, read at LacusCurtius) opens his chapter on the Pisidians "Contiguous to these are the Pisidians" (12.7.1), right after his account of Lycaonia, Isauricê and the Homonadeis: "the greater part of them occupy the summits of the Taurus, but some, situated above Sidê and Aspendus, Pamphylian cities, occupy hilly places" (12.7.1). Of the Pisidian cities Artemidorus lists, "some are entirely in the mountains, while others extend even as far as the foot-hills on either side, to both Pamphylia and Milyas, and border on the Phrygians and the Lydians and the Carians" (12.7.2), and separately, "on the borders of the Phrygians and Caria are situated Tabae and Sinda, and also Amblada" (12.7.2). Since the sentence also names Amblada, in eastern Pisidia, it locates none of the three precisely. Sinda's point (`pleiades:639111`) lies in the south-west, 11 km from Kibyra, and Strabo elsewhere groups "Greater Cibyra and Sinda and Cabalis" (13.4.15). The International Standard Bible Encyclopedia (`bib:isbe-pisidia`) gives the same shape in one sentence: Pisidia was "the huge block of mountain country stretching northward from the Taurus range where the latter overlooked the Pamphylian coast land, to the valleys which connected Apamea with Antioch, and Antioch with Iconium. It was bounded by Lycia on the West, by the Phrygian country on the North, and by Isauria on the East; but there is no natural boundary between Pisidia and Isauria, and the frontier was never strictly drawn."

**No coast, no named river edge.** Pisidia is wholly inland; every edge below is an anchor-pair line, not a coast or a river.

The Kibyratis's `reprPoint` lies 13.6 km from Sinda, and with it as the only outside anchor, Kibyra itself (`pleiades:638804`) would fall inside Pisidia, so this note uses Kibyra instead. **A genuine disagreement: is Antioch itself Pisidian?** The Roman writer Pliny the Elder (`bib:pliny-natural-history`, 5.24, read at Perseus) writes: "The Pisidae, formerly called the Solymi, occupy the higher parts of the mountains. In their country there is the colony of Caesarea, also called Antiochia, and the towns of Oroanda and Sagalessos" — placing Antioch *inside* Pisidia. Strabo and ISBE disagree. In Strabo's Amyntas narrative (12.6.4), the Cilicians and Pisidians "who from the Taurus were overrunning this country, which belonged to the Phrygians and the Cilicians" (the Loeb critical note reads "Lycaonians") threatened Amyntas's lands round "the Antiocheia near Pisidia", and 12.8.14 sets Antioch by Phrygia Paroreia. ISBE Pisidia says Antioch "lay in Galatian Phrygia, near the northern border of Pisidia", and that it took its epithet "the Pisidian" from its proximity to Pisidia and from the military system based on it, not from lying inside it. This note does not resolve the disagreement; it recommends keeping the existing convention (Antioch outside Pisidia, inside Phrygia/Galatia) and naming Pliny's rival reading in the outline's note.

**Confidence:** low. Every edge rests on named towns on both sides from Strabo and ISBE, which is enough to anchor a Voronoi line, but neither author states a line, and ISBE says so outright for the Isauria edge. **Sources:** `bib:strabo-geography` (12.6.2, 12.6.4, 12.7.1–2, 12.8.13–14, 13.4.15, 13.4.17, 14.3.9, 14.4.1–2), `bib:pliny-natural-history` (5.24), `bib:isbe-pisidia`, `bib:isbe-phrygia`, plus the Pleiades IDs above and the existing records named.

**Crossing provinces.** This outline crosses `galatia`, `pamphylia` and `lycia` (Sinda and Kibyra lie in the drawn `lycia`). M5-19's G4.1 already ruled this acceptable (as it did for Phrygia, answer 5), and the PO accepted it (M5-19 §10); §14 question 1 is settled.

**Period:** holds 4 BC – AD 100; no source read here describes Pisidia's own extent changing (only its provincial holder, in AD 74, already covered by `pisidia.json`'s own history).

## 4. Lycaonia

**Extent.** Strabo (`bib:strabo-geography`, 12.6.1, read at LacusCurtius) places Lycaonia as the cold upland plain east of Iconium, with "two lakes in this region, the larger being Lake Coralis and the smaller Lake Trogitis," and names the two places the boundary with Cappadocia lies between: "The boundary between the Lycaonians and the Cappadocians lies between Coropassus, a village of the Lycaonians, and Garsaüra, a town of the Cappadocians. The distance between these strongholds is about one hundred and twenty stadia." He places Isauricê, with Old and New Isaura, as Lycaonia's own district "near the Taurus itself" (12.6.2), and Derbe "closer to Cappadocia than to any other country" (12.6.3). The International Standard Bible Encyclopedia (`bib:isbe-lycaonia`) gives a full circuit: "bounded on the North by Galatia proper..., on the East by Cappadocia, on the South by Cilicia Tracheia, and on the West by Pisidia and Phrygia. The boundary of Phrygia and Lycaonia passed between Iconium and Lystra." This map's "Lycaonia" is the western, Roman part only (Lystra and Derbe, per Acts 14:6, `scripture:Acts 14:6`), excluding Lycaonia Antiochiana, which Rome placed under the client king Antiochus of Commagene from AD 37 (ISBE, already cited by `lycaonia.json`). Before AD 37 that eastern part was not yet separate, so this scope is a convention held for the whole 4 BC – AD 100 window, not a change at AD 37.

**No coast; the Taurus is a range, not a sharp line, on the south.**

The Orcaorci/Pitnissus district is not an inside anchor. It has no Pleiades or Wikidata id; Strabo lists "the regions round Orcaorci and Pitnissus, as also the plateaus of the Lycaonians" side by side (12.6.1); and he gives the Galatian Tectosages "the parts near Greater Phrygia in the neighbourhood of Pessinus and Orcaorci" (12.5.2). Lake Tatta's own side is disputed. Strabo counts it in Greater Phrygia (12.5.4), while 12.8.13 has Greater Phrygia leave Pessinus, Orcaorci and Lycaonia "on the left". **Per PO-3, Lake Tatta lies in neither outline**; both Lycaonia's and Phrygia's notes give both passages.

**Iconium: a three-way disagreement.** Strabo's own Lycaonian-plateau passage (12.6.1) names Iconium in the same breath as the Lycaonian lakes ("In this neighbourhood is also Iconium"). Pliny (`bib:pliny-natural-history`, 5.25, read at Perseus) goes further, placing it inside "a Tetrarchy of Lycaonia... with the famous city of Iconium." ISBE Lycaonia instead draws the Phrygia/Lycaonia line "between Iconium and Lystra," which the project's existing records (`iconium.json`, parented to Galatia directly, and `phrygia.json`) already follow, consistent with Acts 14:6 naming Lystra and Derbe, not Iconium, among "the cities of Lycaonia." **Per the M5-19 ruling P5, Iconium stays outside Lycaonia, on Phrygia's side**; both outlines' notes name Strabo 12.6.1 and Pliny 5.25 as the other reading.

**The record's own point.** `lycaonia.json`'s point (33°E 38°N) lies 46.9 km from Iconium, but 73 km from Lystra and 77 km from Coropassus, the nearest Lycaonian anchors, so an equidistant build gives it to Phrygia. No passage read in either round of this research gives a sourced Lycaonian anchor nearer to the point than Iconium. **Per PO-4, since none is found, the point is named as an exception to the GIS Engineer's check (b)**, the way M5-19 named Cauda and the Sea of Galilee's label point as exceptions to its own checks.

**Confidence:** low overall; **medium** for the east edge specifically, since Strabo gives a named pair of strongholds and a distance (about 120 stadia, which matches the roughly 21.6–21.7 km Pleiades gives between Coropassus and Garsaura) rather than only two towns. The north edge is the weakest: Coropassus is the only Lycaonian anchor near it, and no source gives a line. **Sources:** `bib:strabo-geography` (12.5.2, 12.5.4, 12.6.1–3, 12.8.13), `bib:pliny-natural-history` (5.25), `bib:isbe-lycaonia`, `scripture:Acts 14:6`, plus the Pleiades IDs above.

**One existing-data note, not for this card to fix:** the already-drawn `galatia`/`cappadocia` line leaves Koropassos about 4 km inside Cappadocia, the opposite side from Strabo's own placement (first flagged by the M5-19 Fact-Checker, P9(b)). The new east edge above anchors directly on Strabo's pair rather than reusing that stretch of the existing line.

**Crossing provinces.** Lycaonia's own anchors lie in three provinces: `galatia` (Lystra, Derbe), `pamphylia` (Isaura Vetus) and `cappadocia` (Koropassos, 4.2 km inside it, P9(b)), not "inside `galatia`" alone. M5-19's §4.5 already drew it that way, and the PO accepted it; §14 question 1 is settled here too.

**Period:** holds 4 BC – AD 100; the region's own political split (Lycaonia Antiochiana, AD 37) is already excluded by this map's western-Lycaonia-only scope, and moves no edge of the land this note outlines.

## 5. Mysia

**Extent.** Strabo (`bib:strabo-geography`, 12.8.1, 8, 10–12, read at LacusCurtius) divides Mysia into "Olympenê, which is continuous with Bithynia and Phrygia Epictetus," and "the country in the neighbourhood of the Caïcus River and Pergamenê, extending as far as Teuthrania and the outlets of the river" (12.8.1), and gives it a direct overall reach: "Mysia extends in the interior from Olympenê to Pergamenê, and to the plain of Caïcus... and therefore it lies between Mt. Ida and Catacecaumenê" (12.8.12). The International Standard Bible Encyclopedia (`bib:isbe-mysia`) gives the same reach from the other direction: "it may be said to have extended on the North to the Sea of Marmora[,] on the East to Bithynia and Phrygia, on the South to Lydia, and on the West to Hellespont. According to some authors it included the Troad." The PO's existing M5-19 ruling (§10, answer 6) already accepts including the Troad here, at low confidence, with the dispute named; this note follows that ruling rather than reopening it.

**One edge is a coast.** The Propontis, the Hellespont and (with the Troad included) the Aegean as far south as the outlets of the Caicus bound Mysia on the north and west. Its east end is the Myrleians' country, which is Bithynian (12.8.10, 12.4.3); its south end is the Caicus outlets (12.8.1). No anchor pair is needed between them, only the coastline (`data/geo/ancient-coastline.geojson` and Natural Earth's land). Media and (per §9) the Parthian Empire also have coastal edges.

**Two genuine disagreements.**
1. **Is the Pergamene coast "Mysia" or "Aeolis"?** The project's existing records follow ISBE, not Strabo: `adramyttium.json` and `assos.json` call their cities Mysian, citing `bib:isbe-adramyttium` and `bib:isbe-assos`, and are parented to `mysia`. Pliny's Aeolis reaches north to Cape Lectum and takes in Adramyttium and Assos too (5.32). Strabo's Mysia "lies between Mt. Ida and Catacecaumenê" (12.8.12), but includes Pergamenê and the Caicus country "as far as Teuthrania and the outlets of the river" (12.8.1). This note recommends ISBE's reading for the coast and Strabo's for the Caicus country. Elaea, Pergamon's port at the Caicus mouth, is listed as disputed, and the outline's note names Pliny's Aeolis. **Per PO-6, Elaea stays inside Mysia by default**, following Strabo's Caicus reading, named as disputed (Pliny 5.32; Pleiades' own description calls it "an ancient place in Aeolis").
2. **Cadi.** Strabo lists Cadi among the cities of Phrygia Epictetus, then gives the other view: "and also Cadi, which, according to some writers, belongs to Mysia" (12.8.12). Following M5-19's G6.4 ("Kadoi lies on the edge"), the shared Mysia–Phrygia line passes through Cadi, so it is a vertex of that line rather than an anchor of either side. **Per PO-5, if the build can't pass the line through Cadi, it goes on Phrygia's side**, the side of Strabo's own list, not Mysia's. Both outlines' notes give both readings.

**Confidence:** low throughout. The coastal edge is Mysia's one natural feature, but even it isn't "high," since the Troad's inclusion is itself disputed. **Sources:** `bib:strabo-geography` (12.4.1, 3, 5–6, 12.8.1–2, 10–12), `bib:pliny-natural-history` (5.32), `bib:isbe-mysia`, `scripture:Acts 16:7`, `scripture:Acts 16:8`, plus the Pleiades/Wikidata IDs above.

**One existing-data note, not for this card to fix:** Prusa lies inside the drawn `asia` area, on the wrong side of Strabo's own line (first flagged by the M5-19 Fact-Checker, P9(c)); this is why the east edge above anchors directly on Cyzicus, Miletopolis and Apollonia ad Rhyndacum rather than reusing the existing `asia`/`bithynia` province border.

**Period:** holds 4 BC – AD 100; `mysia.json`'s own history (inside the province of Asia from 129 BC) is unaffected.

## 6. Phrygia

**Extent.** Phrygia is a physical and cultural region with no political area of its own on this map (`phrygia.json` carries no `politicalAreaId`, by design), so its outline is expected to cross province lines, as the PO's M5-19 ruling (§10, answer 5) already accepts. Strabo (`bib:strabo-geography`, 12.8.1, 13–17, read at LacusCurtius) divides it into "Greater Phrygia, the part over which Midas reigned," and "Lesser Phrygia, that on the Hellespont and round Olympus... Phrygia Epictetus." He places Greater Phrygia's own neighbours directly: "Above Phrygia Epictetus towards the south is Greater Phrygia, which leaves on the left Pessinus and the region of Orcaorci and Lycaonia, and on the right the Maeonians and Lydians and Carians" (12.8.13). Pliny (`bib:pliny-natural-history`, 5.41, read at Perseus) gives five neighbours: "On its northern side it borders upon Galatia, on the south it joins Lycaonia, Pisidia, and Mygdonia, and, on the east, it touches upon Cappadocia" (Perseus prints "Lyeaonia"; the quotation above corrects it). His compass points do not all match Strabo's (Galatia and Cappadocia lie east and north-east of Phrygia, and Strabo's Mygdonians live by Cyzicus, 12.8.10), so this note uses Pliny's named neighbours, not his compass points. **No coast; the Maeander is a Phrygian river, not Phrygia's own edge.**

## 7. Media

**Extent.** The Roman writer Pliny the Elder (`bib:pliny-natural-history`, 6.29, read at Perseus) gives a direct boundary: "It has, then, on the east, the Caspii and the Parthi; on the south, Sittacene, Susiane, and Persis; on the west, Adsiabene; and on the north, Armenia." His preceding words, that Media "closes the entrance of both kingdoms into which it is divided", refer to Parthia's Upper and Lower kingdoms (Bostock and Riley's note 9: "The Upper and the Lower, as already mentioned"), not to Media's own two parts. Those parts are Strabo's and ISBE's: Strabo (`bib:strabo-geography`, 11.13.1, 6, read at LacusCurtius) says Media "is divided into two parts. One part of it is called Greater Media... The other part is Atropatian Media" (11.13.1), and ISBE says Media "included Atropatene ... to the North, and Media Magna to the South." Greater Media "is bounded on the east by Parthia and the mountains of the Cossaei", and "also by the Paraetaceni, who border on the Persians"; "on the north by the Cadusii who live above the Hyrcanian Sea"; "on the south by Apolloniatis, which the ancients called Sitacenê, and by the mountain Zagrus, at the place where Massabaticê is situated, which belongs to Media, though some say that it belongs to Elymaea"; "and on the west by the Atropatii and certain of the Armenians" (11.13.6). The International Standard Bible Encyclopedia (`bib:isbe-media`) gives a third compass reading, agreeing on every *named* neighbour while disagreeing with both ancient authors on which side some of them fall: Media "Lay to the West and Southwest of the Caspian, and extended thence to the Zagrus Mountains on the West[;] On the North in later times it was bounded by the rivers Araxes and Cyrus, which separated it from Armenia[;] Its eastern boundaries were formed by Hyrcania and the Great Salt Desert... bounded on the South by Susiana." ISBE adds that "The chief cities of ancient Media were Ecbatana, Gazaea, and Ragae." This note treats the three authorities' *named* neighbours as settled and their compass points as the loose, sometimes contradictory sense ancient and even early-modern geographers gave them, exactly as the M5-19 note already did for this region.

**One edge is a coast.** Media, with Atropatene, reaches the south-west shore of the Caspian. ISBE says Media "Lay to the West and Southwest of the Caspian", and "Near the Caspian the country is low, damp and unhealthy". In Strabo, Atropatene "lies adjacent to the region round the recess of the Hyrcanian Sea" (11.13.2); its northern part is "the abode of the mountaineers called Cadusii, Amardi, Tapyri, Cyrtii" (11.13.3); and the Cadusii "live above the Hyrcanian Sea" (11.13.6). `media.json` already says "beside the Caspian Sea, on its western and southwestern sides". The coast runs from the mouth of the Araxes and the Cyrus to Media's eastern end. That end is disputed, like Rhagae: the Tapyri "formerly belonged to Media" (11.9.1). The Araxes (north-west) is a river edge and the Zagros (south) a range; every other edge is anchor pairs.

## 8. Mesopotamia

**Extent.** Strabo (`bib:strabo-geography`, 16.1.21, read at LacusCurtius) gives Mesopotamia's own definition directly: "it lies between the Euphrates and the Tigris; and the Tigris washes its eastern side only, whereas the Euphrates washes its western and southern sides; and on the north is the Taurus, which separates Armenia from Mesopotamia." He adds that it begins, going south from Commagene, at "the Zeugma... where Mesopotamia begins" (16.1.22), and that it "contracts in shape... and the shape of it somewhat resembles that of a boat," widest "towards the mountains" and narrowest near Babylon, "the shortest distance between the two rivers" lying "somewhere in the neighbourhood of Seleuceia and Babylon, slightly more than two hundred stadia" (16.1.21). He calls the country round Babylon and Seleuceia "Babylonia" (16.1.16). The Roman writer Pliny the Elder (`bib:pliny-natural-history`, 6.30, read at Perseus) gives a broader Mesopotamia that takes in Babylonia: it "contains... Seleucia, Laodicea, Artemita", and he lists Hipparenum among "the following towns in Mesopotamia" and Orchenus "in the same district". His Oruros sentence is a statement about Rome's limit under Pompey, not the first-century boundary: Oruros "under Pompeius Magnus formed the extreme limit of the Roman Empire, distant from Zeugma two hundred and fifty miles" ; Strabo gives the boundary of his own day plainly elsewhere: "The Euphrates and the land beyond it constitute the boundary of the Parthian empire" (16.1.28). This project's own existing record (`mesopotamia.json`) already cites the second-century historian Arrian for the narrower, "Jezira" sense of the name.

**Two edges are rivers; one is a range; the south closes at Strabo's own narrows.**

## 9. The Parthian Empire — not outlined (PO-1)

**Per PO-1, the Parthian Empire is not outlined.** Its east edge has no sourced line in any source read in either round of this research, and the sources disagree on how much of Bactria the empire held, so the card's "not outlinable, with the reason" applies. The empire keeps its name only (ADR-0050 item 5); Media and Mesopotamia, its own child places, are still outlined (§7, §8). The rest of this section keeps the empire's sourced west, north and south edges as research, for the record and for any later card that revisits the question.

**Extent.** Pliny (`bib:pliny-natural-history`, 6.29, read at Perseus) gives the whole empire's compass points directly: its eighteen kingdoms "lie... along the Red Sea to the south, and the Hyrcanian to the north" (ancient usage: "Red Sea" means the Persian Gulf here, not today's sea of that name), with the eleven "Higher provinces" beginning "at the frontiers of Armenia and the shores of the Caspian" and extending "to the Scythians," and the seven "Lower provinces" "bounded" by "the river Euphrates." Strabo (`bib:strabo-geography`, 11.9.2, 16.1.19, 28, read at LacusCurtius) agrees on the western edge in two places: of the dynasty's own rise, "they... established themselves as lords of the whole country inside the Euphrates" and took "a part of Bactriana" too (11.9.2); and plainly, later, "The Euphrates and the land beyond it constitute the boundary of the Parthian empire" (16.1.28). He also says plainly that "the Parthians rule over the Medes and the Babylonians, but they have never once ruled over the Armenians" (16.1.19). The International Standard Bible Encyclopedia (`bib:isbe-parthians`) gives a phrase, not a line, for the eastern edge: "This empire stretched from the Euphrates to the confines of India and the Oxus." Its "Oxus" would take in all of Bactria, which lies south of that river (Strabo 11.11.2), while Strabo (11.9.2, 11.11.2) and ISBE itself (Mithridates I "added to his dominions a part of Bactria") give the Parthians only part of it.

## 10. Rough areas, as a check

The card asks for each region's rough area "from the sources or modern scholarship, as a check" — the direct lesson of the failed attempt (a Media of 3.6 million km², a Mesopotamia reduced to a thin strip). These figures are **checks on the GIS Engineer's eventual shape, not facts about the land, and must stay out of every shipped outline's own note** (M5-19's own practice; confirmed again by PO-8's and the Fact-Checker's S8).

**Media.** Strabo gives Media's own breadth as "four thousand one hundred stadia" between the Zagrus pass and the Caspian Gates, and says its "length and breadth are approximately equal" (11.13.8). At roughly 0.185 km per stadium (the conversion the Coropassus–Garsaura distance confirms: about 120 stadia is about 21.6–21.7 km by Pleiades' own points), that is about 760 km, and a square of that size is about 575,000 km² — the ceiling the Fact-Checker's S1 already accepted, which stands. The report's R47 also asks for a floor, which S1 found missing: the triangle of Media's three settled inside anchors (Ecbatana, Gazaca and Rhagae) is about 41,600 km², from this note's own hull of their coordinates. **Range for the check: about 41,600–575,000 km²**, a wide range reflecting the genuinely uncertain east edge (Rhagae disputed) and the fact that three points make only a floor, not a true lower bound.

**Mesopotamia.** Strabo's own figures can't be multiplied: Thapsacus to Babylon, "four thousand eight hundred stadia" (16.1.22), is a distance along the river, and "two thousand four hundred stadia" (16.1.21) is the widest separation, "towards the mountains". Measured instead between the pinned Natural Earth rivers, closed in the south from Babylon to Seleucia (PO-8) and in the north by the line equidistant from Edessa, Carrhae and Nisibis inside and Arsamosata outside (§8), the land is about 149,000 km² (the Fact-Checker's 0.02° grid). Closed in the north through Nisibis and Edessa instead, it is about 122,000–124,000 km². **Range for the check: 100,000–180,000 km²**; the ceiling is about 1.2 times the estimate, because both long edges are pinned rivers and the south close is fixed.

**Pisidia and Lycaonia.** The hull of four Pisidian towns (Selge, Sagalassus, Termessus, Cremna) is 2,199 km², a floor. The hull of Lycaonia's own inside anchors is 7,375 km², also a floor (Lystra, Derbe, Coropassus and Isaura Vetus; not Iconium, which is outside). The sources give neither region a ceiling. A hull widened to the outside anchors isn't one either, because an equidistant cell reaches beyond its anchors. Each ceiling therefore comes from the Fact-Checker's grid estimate (0.02°) of the equidistant build from the corrected anchors: Pisidia about 21,600 km²; Lycaonia about 17,200 km² with the drawn Cilicia areas excluded (§4), or 26,400 km² without them. **Range for the check: Pisidia about 2,200–32,000 km²** (1.5 times the estimate); **Lycaonia about 7,400–43,000 km²** (kept; about 1.6 times the unclipped estimate).

**Mysia and Phrygia.** The report's R47 asks that each region "come out at tens of thousands of km²" and that this note "give each a numeric floor and ceiling with its basis, as for Pisidia and Lycaonia, so that the GIS check can test them." This note does so directly, by the same hull method, from this round's confirmed anchor coordinates. **Mysia:** the hull of its inside anchors (Cyzicus, Miletopolis, Apollonia ad Rhyndacum, Pergamon, Teuthrania, Elaea, and the Troad towns Troas, Assos and Adramyttium) is about 16,000 km², a floor; the hull of every anchor this note lists for Mysia, inside and outside together — a generous, rough ceiling, since a true equidistant line sits inside it — is about 56,700 km². **Range for the check: about 16,000–57,000 km².** The Fact-Checker's grid estimate, on the Asian mainland, is about 29,500 km². **Phrygia:** the hull of its inside anchors (the Phrygia Epictetus towns, Synnada, Amorium, Philomelium, Antioch-near-Pisidia, Iconium, Laodicea, Colossae and Apamea Cibotus) is about 40,000 km², a floor; the combined inside-and-outside hull is about 89,600 km², but that is not a true ceiling, since an equidistant cell reaches beyond its anchors. The Fact-Checker's grid estimate (0.02°) of the equidistant build is about 78,200 km². **Range for the check: about 40,000–117,000 km²** (1.5 times the estimate). Both ranges land at tens of thousands of km², as the correction expects, for regions that are each a corner of, or split between, provinces already drawn at `asia` (about 133,000 km²) and `galatia` (about 77,000 km²).

**The neighbours, for comparison:** `pamphylia` about 23,500 km², `lycia` about 15,100 km², `galatia` about 77,000 km², `cappadocia` about 110,000 km², `bithynia` about 66,000 km², `asia` about 134,000 km² — each a fraction of modern Türkiye's own 783,000 km².

**The Parthian Empire.** Per PO-1, the empire is not outlined, so it needs no area range.
