# Damascus — research brief for AI-generated reconstructions

Period shown: about AD 33–37, the years around Paul's conversion near Damascus and his early preaching there (Acts 9:1-25), when the city was for a time under Nabataean rather than direct Roman control.

## Setting and layout

Damascus lies in the north-west corner of the Ghuta, a fertile plain about 2,300 ft above sea level, watered by the river Barada (the biblical Abana), and surrounded on three sides by bare hills, with the desert along its open eastern side [bib:isbe-damascus; pleiades:678106; wikidata:Q3678596]. ISBE describes the city of its own day as about a mile long from east to west and half a mile broad [bib:isbe-damascus]. UNESCO notes that the old city keeps the Roman street plan, laid out on north–south and east–west lines [bib:unesco-damascus].

## What stood then

Already one of the world's most ancient cities still lived in today [bib:unesco-damascus], Damascus came under Rome when Pompey annexed Syria in 64 BC; its coins bear the names of Augustus or Tiberius from 31 BC to AD 33, and afterwards it was again in Nabataean hands, ruled by an ethnarch, or governor, appointed by King Aretas [bib:isbe-damascus; bib:livius-damascus]. Paul writes that "the governor under King Aretas guarded the Damascenes' city, desiring to arrest me" (2 Corinthians 11:32), and Acts says his opponents "watched the gates both day and night" [scripture:2 Corinthians 11:32; scripture:Acts 9:24]. This Aretas is the king Livius lists as Aretas IV (9 BC – AD 40) [bib:isbe-aretas; bib:livius-nabataeans]. In the Greek period a long colonnaded street ran through the city, which ISBE identifies with "the street which is called Straight" (Acts 9:11); it runs westward from the East Gate, and remains of columns have been found along its course [bib:isbe-damascus; bib:livius-damascus; scripture:Acts 9:11]. UNESCO notes that large parts of the Roman Temple of Jupiter, some gates and a long stretch of the Roman city walls survive [bib:unesco-damascus].

## Materials and colours

ISBE describes the houses of the early-20th-century city as flat-roofed and usually built round a courtyard with a fountain [bib:isbe-damascus]; the sources used here do not describe 1st-century houses, so this is a guide only. Part of the city wall stands on Roman foundations [bib:isbe-damascus].

## Landscape and plants

The Barada spreads in many channels over the plain, and the Ghuta, with its streams and fountains, fields and orchards (apricots, pomegranates, walnuts and many others, as ISBE describes them), is praised in Arabic literature as an earthly paradise; bare hills rise beyond the green plain [bib:isbe-damascus].

## People and daily life

ISBE says Damascus "has always been a manufacturing city" and calls it "the natural harbor of the Syrian desert" [bib:isbe-damascus]. Paul came bearing letters to "the synagogues of Damascus," implying an organized resident Jewish community there, among whom he began to preach almost immediately after his sight was restored (Acts 9:2, 9:20) [bib:isbe-damascus; scripture:Acts 9:2; scripture:Acts 9:20].

## Key sites to show

1. The Barada river and its orchard oasis set against the desert backdrop [bib:isbe-damascus].
2. The colonnaded Straight Street running through the city [bib:isbe-damascus; scripture:Acts 9:11].
3. A city wall and gate, evoking Paul's escape, "let down in a basket through a window by the wall" (2 Corinthians 11:33) [scripture:2 Corinthians 11:33].
4. A flat-roofed courtyard-house quarter [bib:isbe-damascus].

## Not known

No source used here gives a block-by-block reconstruction of mid-1st-century Damascus, since its ancient street plan lies beneath the much later Old City; beyond Straight Street's course and the oasis setting, building façades should stay generic Greco-Roman Syrian rather than copy any surviving but much later structure, such as the Great Mosque, built under the Umayyad caliphs on the site of a Roman temple, or the present city walls, which date from the Middle Ages [bib:isbe-damascus; bib:unesco-damascus]. The place of the escape shown to visitors today rests on tradition [bib:isbe-damascus], so no surviving gate or chapel should stand in for the 1st-century wall.
