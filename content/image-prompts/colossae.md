# Colossae — research brief for AI-generated reconstructions

Colossae is barely excavated: its mound near modern Honaz drew only short inspections until a survey began in 2021, and full-scale excavation has only just begun; no building stands above ground, and the city's layout is still to be uncovered [pleiades:638811; bib:bas-colossae-yener]. This brief therefore leans on the wider Lycus valley landscape and on the neighboring, better-known cities of Laodicea and Hierapolis only where the sources support that comparison, and keeps Colossae's own buildings generic.

## Setting and layout

Colossae stood in the Lycus valley of Phrygia, near modern Honaz [pleiades:638811; wikidata:Q1001370]. The Lycus joins the Maeander near Laodicea [bib:strabo-geography]. Colossae lies about 14 km east of Laodicea, and Hierapolis about 20 km to its north-west, across the valley [pleiades:638811; dare:21162; dare:21205]. Strabo places Mt Cadmus above Laodicea as the source of the Lycus, which "flows under ground for the most part", a sign that "the country is full of holes and subject to earthquakes" [bib:strabo-geography]. Which modern mountain is Cadmus is not settled by the sources used here: ISBE puts Colossae 3 miles from Mt Cadmus [bib:isbe-colossae], while Pleiades identifies Cadmus with Babadağ [pleiades:638884]. Images should not label any mountain as Cadmus.

## What stood then

Strabo counts Apameia and Laodicea as the largest cities of Phrygia and lists Colossae among the towns around them [bib:strabo-geography]; ISBE notes that Colossae declined as its neighbors grew [bib:isbe-colossae]. For centuries local people took stone from the mound for building [bib:bas-colossae-yener]. He says the Colossians drew revenue from a wool colour that bears their name (the Loeb translator's note: dyed purple or madder-red), as the Laodiceans did from the raven-black wool of their sheep [bib:strabo-geography]. The whole district was earthquake-prone: Strabo names Laodicea and the village of Carura, which also has boiling-hot springs, as especially subject to earthquakes [bib:strabo-geography], and Tacitus records that an earthquake destroyed Laodicea itself in AD 60, which the city rebuilt from its own resources without help from Rome [bib:tacitus-annals]. Whether this quake, or another like it, also struck Colossae and contributed to its later obscurity is debated and not settled by the sources used here.

## Materials and colours

Hierapolis, across the valley, is famous for the white travertine terraces formed by its mineral hot springs, which UNESCO lists together with the city's ruins [bib:unesco-hierapolis-pamukkale]. No excavation report describes Colossae's own building materials, so its structures should not borrow Hierapolis's white terraces or any specific excavated building from Laodicea.

## Landscape and plants

The country round Laodicea produced sheep famous for the softness and raven-black colour of their wool [bib:strabo-geography]. The sources used here do not describe the valley's crops or trees, so they should stay generic.

## People and daily life

Paul writes of his struggle "for you and for those at Laodicea, and for as many as have not seen my face in the flesh" (Colossians 2:1) [scripture:Colossians 2:1]. Any figures shown should be generic valley villagers, shepherds and wool-workers rather than named individuals.

## Not known

Almost everything about Colossae's 1st-century built environment is unknown: excavation has only just begun, and the city's buildings and layout are still to be uncovered [bib:bas-colossae-yener]. Any depiction of Colossae's own buildings must stay generic and must be kept visually distinct from the well-excavated remains at Laodicea or Hierapolis, which should appear only as clearly labelled neighboring cities, never substituted for Colossae [pleiades:638811; wikidata:Q1001370; bib:unesco-hierapolis-pamukkale].
