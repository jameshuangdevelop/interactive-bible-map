# Colossae — research brief for AI-generated reconstructions

Colossae is barely excavated: its mound (höyük) near modern Honaz has never been systematically dug, so no excavated street, building, or plan exists to draw from [pleiades:638811; wikidata:Q1001370]. This brief therefore leans on the wider Lycus valley landscape and on the neighboring, better-known cities of Laodicea and Hierapolis only where the sources support that comparison, and keeps Colossae's own buildings generic.

## Setting and layout

Colossae stood in the Lycus river valley of Phrygia, a tributary valley of the Maeander, near the foot of Mt Cadmus, between the larger cities of Laodicea and Hierapolis [pleiades:638811; wikidata:Q1001370; bib:strabo-geography]. Strabo, writing shortly before Paul's time, records that the Lycus rises on Mt Cadmus and flows underground for much of its course before resurfacing, a sign, in his words, that "the country is full of holes and subject to earthquakes" [bib:strabo-geography].

## What stood then

By Paul's day Colossae was already a lesser town overshadowed by its neighbors. Strabo names it only for a reddish-black wool dye, "the colour which bears the same name" as the town, traded alongside the famous raven-black wool of nearby Laodicea [bib:strabo-geography]. The whole district was earthquake-prone: Strabo singles out Laodicea and the village of Carura nearby as especially subject to tremors, hot springs, and underground fire [bib:strabo-geography], and Tacitus records that an earthquake destroyed Laodicea itself in AD 60, which the city rebuilt from its own resources without help from Rome [bib:tacitus-annals]. Whether this quake, or another like it, also struck Colossae and contributed to its later obscurity is debated and not settled by the sources used here.

## Materials and colours

Hierapolis, across the valley, is famous for the brilliant white travertine terraces formed by its mineral hot springs, a UNESCO-recognized natural and cultural landscape [bib:unesco-hierapolis-pamukkale]. No excavation report describes Colossae's own building materials, so its structures should not borrow Hierapolis's white terraces or any specific excavated building from Laodicea.

## Landscape and plants

The Lycus valley is fertile river-bottom land well suited to sheep-grazing and wool-dyeing, framed by the slopes of Mt Cadmus [bib:strabo-geography].

## People and daily life

Paul had not personally visited the town he addressed in his letter: he writes to the Colossians as people "who have not seen my face in the flesh" (Colossians 2:1), so any figures shown should be generic Phrygian valley villagers and weavers rather than named individuals [scripture:Colossians 2:1].

## Key sites to show

1. The still-unexcavated mound of Colossae itself, shown honestly as a grassy, eroded tell in its valley setting, not as a reconstructed city [pleiades:638811; wikidata:Q1001370].
2. The wider Lycus valley landscape with Mt Cadmus on the skyline [bib:strabo-geography].
3. Hierapolis's white travertine terraces across the valley, clearly labelled as the neighboring city, never as Colossae itself [bib:unesco-hierapolis-pamukkale].
4. Sheep-grazing and wool-dyeing imagery evoking the valley's textile trade [bib:strabo-geography].

## Not known

Almost everything about Colossae's 1st-century built environment is unknown: no temple, forum, or street has been excavated and published. Any depiction of Colossae's own buildings must stay generic and must be kept visually distinct from the well-excavated remains at Laodicea or Hierapolis, which should appear only as clearly labelled neighboring cities, never substituted for Colossae [pleiades:638811; wikidata:Q1001370; bib:unesco-hierapolis-pamukkale].
