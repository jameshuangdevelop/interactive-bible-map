# Pergamum — research brief for AI-generated reconstructions

## Setting and layout
Pergamon stood on a steep, cone-shaped hill rising sharply from the Caicus (Bakırçay) plain in Mysia, in the region of modern Bergama. Strabo describes the acropolis as ending in a sharp peak, settled "on the very summit of the mountain," with the Caicus river flowing past through a notably fertile plain [bib:strabo-geography]. Founded as a Hellenistic royal capital, Pergamon passed to Rome in 133 BC and became the first capital of the province of Asia, with Ephesus only later overtaking it as administrative centre [bib:unesco-pergamon; wikidata:Q18986]. By the second half of the first century AD, the monumental acropolis — built up over two centuries by the Attalid kings and maintained under Rome — crowned the hill, with residential and civic quarters on the lower slopes and the plain below.

## What stood then
Much of the famous acropolis complex was already centuries old by AD 95. The Great Altar of Zeus Soter, with its long sculpted frieze of gods battling giants, was built under Eumenes II in the second century BC and stood as the acropolis's most celebrated monument [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; bib:strabo-geography]. The steep Hellenistic theatre, one of the steepest in the ancient world, and the sanctuary of Athena with its library — once one of antiquity's largest, rivalling Alexandria's — also dated to the Attalid kings, Eumenes II in particular having "added sacred buildings and libraries" according to Strabo [bib:strabo-geography]. The sanctuary of Asklepios, the healing god, existed below the acropolis from the Hellenistic period, though its grand round temple visible in ruins today was built under Hadrian, in the second century AD, after our period [bib:worldhistory-pergamon]. Likewise the Trajaneum (temple of Trajan and Zeus Philios) on the acropolis and the Red Basilica-Serapeum complex in the lower city were both built under Trajan and Hadrian in the early second century and had **not** yet been built by AD 95 [bib:worldhistory-pergamon]. Pergamon's standing as a seat of the Roman imperial cult dates to 29 BC, when it was granted the first temple in Asia dedicated to the deified Augustus and the goddess Roma — this earlier temple, standing by AD 95, is one basis for Revelation's description of Pergamum as the place "where Satan's throne is" (Revelation 2:13) [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; scripture:Revelation 2:13]. Scholars differ on the phrase's referent: Ramsay argued for the imperial cult; others point to the Great Altar, visible on its terrace; still others to the Asklepios sanctuary, whose symbol was the serpent. This brief does not choose among them [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; bib:worldhistory-pergamon].

## Materials and colours
The acropolis buildings were of local and imported stone, with marble facings on the grander Hellenistic structures; the Great Altar's reliefs were carved marble. Roofs were tile. The steep hill itself is a pale grey-tan rock, terraced for the Hellenistic palaces, temples, and the theatre's tiers of stone seating. The lower city and plain would show cultivated fields in green and gold along the Caicus.

## Landscape and plants
The acropolis rises dramatically above a broad agricultural plain watered by the Caicus river, described by Strabo as very fertile [bib:strabo-geography]. The surrounding countryside would have shown cultivated fields, olive groves, and scattered trees, with the hill itself largely built up in terraces rather than wooded.

## People and daily life
As the Roman province of Asia's first capital and a seat of the imperial cult, Pergamon's population included Greek-speaking civic elites, Roman officials, and a religious and medical community drawn to the Asklepios sanctuary. Dress followed typical Greco-Roman provincial patterns — tunics and cloaks, with distinctive priestly or cultic dress for imperial-cult ceremonies held at the Augustus temple.

## Key sites to show
- A distant or elevated view of the steep acropolis hill crowned by its Hellenistic temples and theatre, rising above the Caicus plain — the best single "overall feel" shot.
- The Great Altar of Zeus's terrace and the steep theatre cut into the hillside, both standing by AD 95.
- The Augustus and Roma temple site (even if only its general acropolis location is shown, since its precise form is not well preserved).

## Not known
The exact appearance of the 29 BC temple to Augustus and Roma is not well documented archaeologically; it should be shown only in general terms (a modest classical temple on the acropolis), not with invented detail. The Trajaneum and the Red Basilica, though the most photogenic ruins at the site today, are second-century AD and after our period — they should not appear in a reconstruction of the city as it stood when Revelation was written. The precise built form of the Asklepios sanctuary before its Hadrianic rebuilding is likewise not well known and should be kept modest and generic if shown at all.
