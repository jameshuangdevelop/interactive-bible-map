# Smyrna — research brief for AI-generated reconstructions

## Setting and layout
Smyrna sat on the Aegean coast at the head of a gulf, at the site of modern İzmir [bib:strabo-geography; bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. The Hellenistic and Roman city was a refoundation: the old Greek city, on a steep hill about two miles away across the head of the gulf, had been destroyed by the Lydian king Alyattes about 600 BC, and its people lived in scattered villages until Antigonus and then Lysimachus gathered them into a new city, around 290 BC [bib:strabo-geography; bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. By AD 95 this refounded city was roughly three centuries old. Strabo describes an exceptionally good plan of streets laid out in straight lines and paved with stone; part of the city stood on a mountain and was walled, and the greater part lay in the plain near the harbour, the Metroön and the gymnasium [bib:strabo-geography]. Ramsay names the mountain Pagos (Pagus) and describes the city spreading down its rounded slopes [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. The small River Meles ran near the walls, and the harbour could be closed [bib:strabo-geography]. Ramsay explains the local phrase "the crown of Smyrna" as the ring of public buildings on and around the rounded hill, and connects it to Revelation's promise of "the crown of life" (Revelation 2:10) [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; scripture:Revelation 2:10].

## What stood then
Strabo's description, written in the early first century AD (he mentions Tiberius's help after the earthquake of AD 17), some seventy years before Revelation, is the nearest direct evidence: besides the paved streets, the city had large square porticoes with lower and upper storeys, a public library, and the Homereium, a square portico containing a shrine and wooden statue of Homer, whom the Smyrnaeans claimed as their own [bib:strabo-geography]. Strabo does not say that the streets themselves were lined with colonnades [bib:strabo-geography]. In AD 26, eleven Asian cities competed before the Roman Senate for the right to build a temple to the emperor Tiberius; Tacitus records the final contest as Sardis against Smyrna, with Smyrna winning on the strength of its long loyalty to Rome, including a temple to the city of Rome built as early as 195 BC [bib:tacitus-annals]. Tacitus describes it as a temple to Tiberius, his mother and the senate, and Ramsay notes that it was built, as the province's second temple of the imperial cult; the sources read for this brief do not describe its site or appearance [bib:tacitus-annals; bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. Strabo also notes a civic flaw: the paved streets had no underground drainage, so filth covered the surface, especially in rain [bib:strabo-geography]. Ramsay describes a "Street of Gold" that the second-century orator Aristides praised, and links to it a first-century remark by Apollonius of Tyana about Smyrna's "crown" of porticoes and gold; its route, date and appearance are not known [bib:ramsay-seven-churches].

## Materials and colours
Strabo says only that the streets were paved with stone and that the large porticoes had lower and upper storeys [bib:strabo-geography]. The sources read for this brief do not describe Smyrna's building stone, plaster or roofing, so images should keep to the general look of a Hellenistic–Roman city (pale stone public buildings, tiled roofs); this is general guidance, not a sourced detail. The walled upper city on the mountain, the lower city by the harbour, and the gulf give the main shapes of a wide view [bib:strabo-geography; bib:ramsay-seven-churches].

## Landscape and plants
The greater part of the city lay in the plain by the harbour at the head of the gulf, with the mountain rising behind [bib:strabo-geography]. The sources read for this brief do not describe the farmland or trees around Smyrna; ordinary Mediterranean cover (olives, vines, scattered trees) is a reasonable general choice, not a sourced detail.

## People and daily life
Revelation addresses the Smyrnaean church in oppression and poverty, and mentions hostility from "those who say they are Jews" (Revelation 2:9) [scripture:Revelation 2:9]; Ramsay takes the passage as evidence of an organised Jewish community in the city [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. As a port with a closable harbour, and a city long loyal to Rome, Smyrna would have had traders, harbour workers, citizens and Roman officials [bib:strabo-geography; bib:tacitus-annals]. Dress was presumably typical of the Greco-Roman provinces (tunics and cloaks, with togas among some Roman citizens); this is general, not a sourced detail for Smyrna.

## Key sites to show
- A wide view from the gulf or harbour toward Mount Pagus, showing the walled upper city above the lower city's straight streets — the single best "overall feel" shot.
- A straight, stone-paved street opening onto one of the large two-storey square porticoes that Strabo describes.
- The closable harbour itself, a distinguishing feature of the ancient city.

## Not known
The site and appearance of the temple to Tiberius, his mother and the senate are not described by the sources read for this brief, and neither is the look of the Metroön, gymnasium or library that Strabo mentions. Any image should keep these generic or at a distance rather than invented in detail. The "Street of Gold" is known only from figurative descriptions, and its route, date and form are not known, so images should not invent a grand colonnaded avenue: Strabo describes straight paved streets and large square porticoes, not colonnade-lined streets.
