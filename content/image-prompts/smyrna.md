# Smyrna — research brief for AI-generated reconstructions

## Setting and layout
Smyrna sat on the Aegean coast at the head of a sheltered gulf, at the site of modern İzmir. The Hellenistic and Roman city was a refoundation: the original Greek city at nearby Bayraklı had been destroyed by the Lydian king Alyattes around 600 BC, and Alexander's successors Antigonus and then Lysimachus gathered the scattered population into a new city on and below Mount Pagus, facing the gulf, around 290 BC [bib:strabo-geography; bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. By AD 95 this refounded city was roughly three centuries old. Strabo describes an exceptionally regular street grid, paved in stone; part of the city was walled on the mountain, the rest in the plain near the harbour, the Metroön (temple of the Mother goddess), and the gymnasium [bib:strabo-geography]. The small River Meles ran near the walls, and the harbour could be closed off, a notable feature [bib:strabo-geography]. Mount Pagus, crowning the city, gave rise to the local image of a "crown of Smyrna," which Ramsay connects to Revelation's promise of a "crown of life" (Revelation 2:10) [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; scripture:Revelation 2:10].

## What stood then
Strabo's description, written only a few decades before Revelation, is good direct evidence for the second half of the first century: large two-storey porticoes lined the paved streets, a public library stood in the city, and a portico called the Homereium held a shrine and wooden statue of Homer, whom the Smyrnaeans claimed as their own [bib:strabo-geography]. In AD 26, eleven Asian cities competed before the Roman Senate to build a temple to the emperor Tiberius; Tacitus records the final contest as Sardis against Smyrna, with Smyrna winning on the strength of centuries of loyalty to Rome, including a temple to the city of Rome itself as early as 195 BC [bib:tacitus-annals]. The resulting Temple of Tiberius, Livia, and the Senate, built soon after, would have stood as one of the city's grandest monuments by AD 95. Strabo also notes a civic flaw: the paved streets had no underground drainage, so filth collected on the surface in rain [bib:strabo-geography]. The grander "Golden Street" colonnade and temple crescent often associated with Smyrna belong to a later, second-century rebuilding and are not shown here.

## Materials and colours
Public buildings were of stone, with colonnades and porticoes of local and imported marble; streets were stone-paved. Mount Pagus's slopes would show the walled acropolis in pale, weathered masonry above the lower city's grid of flat-roofed, tiled buildings. The gulf and harbour give the strongest colour note: blue water framed by the tan-and-grey mass of Pagus and the surrounding hills, with cultivated green land near the walls.

## Landscape and plants
Smyrna lay on a fertile coastal plain at the head of a gulf, with hills rising directly behind the city. Mediterranean dry-summer vegetation — olives, vines, and scattered trees — would have covered the surrounding slopes and valley floor, with cultivated land close to the walls.

## People and daily life
Revelation addresses the Smyrnaean church under affliction and poverty, and mentions tension with a local synagogue (Revelation 2:9), indicating an organized Jewish community in the city alongside the Christian congregation [scripture:Revelation 2:9]. As a major Aegean port and a loyal ally city of Rome, Smyrna's population would have included Greek-speaking citizens, Roman citizens and officials, traders passing through its closable harbour, and dock and market workers. Dress would be typical Greco-Roman provincial: tunics and cloaks for ordinary residents, with Roman-style togas among citizens of higher status.

## Key sites to show
- A wide view from the gulf or harbour toward Mount Pagus, showing the walled acropolis above the grid of the lower city — the single best "overall feel" shot.
- The paved, colonnaded main street with its two-storey stoas, as Strabo describes.
- The closable harbour itself, a distinguishing feature of the ancient city.

## Not known
The exact appearance and full extent of the Temple of Tiberius built after AD 26 is not well preserved archaeologically; its general location and grandeur, but not its precise architectural detail, are known. The precise look of the Metroön and gymnasium Strabo mentions is likewise undetailed in the sources. Any image should keep these generic or at a distance rather than invented in detail, and should not show the later "Golden Street" colonnade arrangement, which developed after a second-century earthquake rebuilding.
