# *The Temple Mount* — research brief for AI-generated reconstructions

## Setting and layout
The Temple Mount was a roughly 15-hectare raised platform on Jerusalem's eastern ridge, expanded by Herod the Great from a smaller, uneven hilltop by building retaining walls and filling behind them, a project that took "long ages" and exhausted vast sums [bib:josephus-jewish-war; wikidata:Q193163]. Worshippers passed through an outer court open to all nations, then a stone balustrade about three cubits high carrying inscriptions in Greek and Latin warning foreigners not to go further, then fourteen steps up into the Court of the Women, then more steps and gates into the inner courts around the sanctuary itself [bib:josephus-jewish-war; bib:ritmeyer-temple-mount-quest].

## What stood then
Herod began his rebuilding of the Second Temple and platform around 20–19 BC, and the Gospel of John records the project still unfinished decades into the first century AD [bib:ritmeyer-temple-mount-quest; scripture:John 2:20]. The Antonia fortress, a Roman garrison post, stood at the Mount's northwest corner, overlooking the courts below [bib:josephus-jewish-war]. Double colonnaded cloisters ran around the outer court, and the sanctuary building itself rose in two unequal parts behind a towering, doorless front gate [bib:josephus-jewish-war]. The Temple and platform were destroyed by Roman forces under Titus in AD 70, after this period, leaving the retaining walls, including the section known today as the Western Wall, as the most substantial surviving remains [wikidata:Q193163; bib:ritmeyer-temple-mount-quest].

## Materials and colours
The outer cloisters' columns were single blocks of white marble, twenty-five cubits tall, with roofs of carved cedar; the courts themselves were paved with stone [bib:josephus-jewish-war]. Nine of the inner gates were overlaid with gold and silver on their doors, jambs and lintels; one gate, facing the sanctuary, was instead of Corinthian bronze and considered finer still than the gilded ones [bib:josephus-jewish-war]. The sanctuary's great entrance was covered in gold, and above it hung a golden vine with clusters of grapes the height of a man [bib:josephus-jewish-war]. The platform's own foundation stones were exceptionally large, some reported at forty cubits, consistent with the massive Herodian ashlars still visible in the surviving walls [bib:josephus-jewish-war; bib:ritmeyer-temple-mount-quest].

## Landscape and plants
The platform itself held no planted vegetation — it was an open stone precinct — but it overlooked the Kidron Valley and the olive-planted slope of the Mount of Olives to the east [bib:rainey-notley-sacred-bridge].

## People and daily life
Priests in linen vestments carried out daily sacrifices and incense offerings; Levites assisted; pilgrims from across the Jewish world filled the outer court at festivals [scripture:Acts 2:5]. Merchants sold sacrificial animals — oxen, sheep and doves — and money changers exchanged currency for the Temple tax, a trade the Gospels describe Jesus disrupting [scripture:Matthew 21:12; scripture:John 2:14].

## Key sites to show
1. The massive Herodian retaining wall and foundation courses from outside the platform.
2. The outer court's double colonnades with the Antonia fortress visible at the corner.
3. The sanctuary's gold-covered entrance gate with its golden vine.
4. The inscribed balustrade marking the boundary gentiles could not cross.

## Not known
No contemporary image of the sanctuary's interior survives; Josephus's account, written decades later, is the fullest ancient description and cannot be checked against a surviving building [bib:josephus-jewish-war]. The exact number and arrangement of columns in some cloisters, and the precise final decorative scheme, rest on later reconstruction work rather than direct observation [bib:ritmeyer-temple-mount-quest]. Any image should keep the sanctuary's interior out of frame and should not show the Dome of the Rock or other structures built long after this period.
