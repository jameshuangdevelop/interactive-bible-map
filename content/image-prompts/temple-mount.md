# *The Temple Mount* — research brief for AI-generated reconstructions

## Setting and layout
The Temple Mount was a vast raised platform on Jerusalem's eastern ridge, grown over centuries from a smaller, uneven hilltop by building retaining walls from the valley floors and filling behind them — Josephus says the work took "long ages" and exhausted the sacred treasuries — and it was enclosed by cloisters whose outer circuit he gives as six furlongs, including the Antonia [bib:josephus-jewish-war; wikidata:Q193163]. Worshippers passed through an outer court open to all nations, then a stone balustrade three cubits high carrying inscriptions in Greek and Latin warning foreigners not to go further, then fourteen steps up to a level terrace, then five more steps to the gates of the inner courts, including the Court of the Women, around the sanctuary itself [bib:josephus-jewish-war; bib:ritmeyer-temple-mount-quest].

## What stood then
Herod began his rebuilding of the Second Temple and platform in the eighteenth year of his reign, around 20–19 BC; in John's Gospel, Jesus' hearers say it "took forty-six years to build," and Josephus dates its completion to the 60s AD [bib:josephus-antiquities; bib:ritmeyer-temple-mount-quest; scripture:John 2:20]. The Antonia fortress, a Roman garrison post, stood at the Mount's northwest corner, overlooking the courts below [bib:josephus-jewish-war]. Double colonnaded cloisters ran around the outer court, and the sanctuary building itself had a front a hundred cubits high and wide, with a narrower body behind it, and a towering front gate without doors [bib:josephus-jewish-war]. The Temple and platform were destroyed by Roman forces under Titus in AD 70, after this period, leaving the retaining walls, including the section known today as the Western Wall, as the most substantial surviving remains [wikidata:Q193163; bib:ritmeyer-temple-mount-quest].

## Materials and colours
The outer cloisters' columns were single blocks of white marble, twenty-five cubits tall, with roofs of carved cedar; the courts themselves were paved with stone [bib:josephus-jewish-war]. Nine of the inner gates were overlaid with gold and silver on their doors, jambs and lintels; one gate, outside the inner court of the sanctuary on its east side, was instead of Corinthian bronze and considered finer still than the gilded ones [bib:josephus-jewish-war]. The sanctuary's great entrance was covered in gold, and above it hung a golden vine with clusters of grapes the height of a man [bib:josephus-jewish-war]. The platform's own foundation stones were exceptionally large, some reported at forty cubits, consistent with the massive Herodian ashlars still visible in the surviving walls [bib:josephus-jewish-war; bib:ritmeyer-temple-mount-quest].

## Landscape and plants
The platform itself held no planted vegetation — it was an open stone precinct — but it overlooked the Kidron Valley and the olive-planted slope of the Mount of Olives to the east [bib:rainey-notley-sacred-bridge].

## People and daily life
Priests served in turn by their divisions, one chosen by lot to burn incense in the sanctuary [scripture:Luke 1:8-9]; pilgrims — Acts speaks of devout Jews "from every nation under the sky" — filled the city at festivals [scripture:Acts 2:5]. Merchants sold sacrificial animals — oxen, sheep and doves — and money changers sat at their tables in the Temple, a trade the Gospels describe Jesus disrupting [scripture:Matthew 21:12; scripture:John 2:14].

## Key sites to show
1. The massive Herodian retaining wall and foundation courses from outside the platform.
2. The outer court's double colonnades with the Antonia fortress visible at the corner.
3. The sanctuary's gold-covered entrance gate with its golden vine.
4. The inscribed balustrade marking the boundary gentiles could not cross.

## Not known
No contemporary image of the sanctuary's interior survives; Josephus's account, written decades later, is the fullest ancient description and cannot be checked against a surviving building [bib:josephus-jewish-war]. The exact number and arrangement of columns in some cloisters, and the precise final decorative scheme, rest on later reconstruction work rather than direct observation [bib:ritmeyer-temple-mount-quest]. Any image should keep the sanctuary's interior out of frame and should not show the Dome of the Rock or other structures built long after this period.
