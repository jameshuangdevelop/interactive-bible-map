# Laodicea — research brief for AI-generated reconstructions

## Setting and layout
Laodicea on the Lycus stood in the lower part of the Lycus valley, a tributary glen running into the wider valley where Lydia, Phrygia, and Caria meet, at the site of modern Denizli (Eskihisar) [bib:strabo-geography; bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. Mount Cadmus (modern Honaz Dağı) rose above the city to the south, source of the Lycus river, which Strabo notes ran underground for much of its course — one sign, he says, of a region "full of holes and subject to earthquakes" [bib:strabo-geography]. Due north, clearly visible across the valley, stood Hierapolis, famous for its hot springs and a cascade of white mineral cliffs — a landmark still visible today and a defining feature of the view from Laodicea [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; bib:strabo-geography]. Colossae lay further up the valley to the east [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; scripture:Colossians 4:13]. Laodicea stood at a major crossroads, where the east-west road from Ephesus and Miletus met roads north to Pergamon and south toward Caria, making it a wealthy trade city [bib:ramsay-seven-churches].

## What stood then
Laodicea's recent history by AD 95 was dominated by catastrophe and recovery. Tacitus records that in AD 60, under Nero, "Laodicea, one of the famous Asiatic cities, was laid in ruins by an earthquake, but recovered by its own resources, without assistance from ourselves" — rebuilding itself without the imperial relief Rome gave Sardis after the earlier AD 17 earthquake [bib:tacitus-annals]. This self-funded rebuilding is often connected by commentators to Revelation's rebuke of the Laodicean church for saying "I am rich...and have need of nothing" (Revelation 3:17), though Revelation does not state the connection [scripture:Revelation 3:17; bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. By AD 95, some 35 years after the earthquake, the city was likely still an active building site: excavations directed by Pamukkale University have uncovered colonnaded streets, a stadium, two theatres, baths, a bouleuterion, temples, and a large agora, but much of this — including the grandest colonnades and the Nymphaeum associated with Septimius Severus — dates to the second century AD or later [bib:pau-laodikeia-kazisi]. A reconstruction should show a city in transition: some quarters finished, others under repair. Its water came by underground aqueduct from springs to the south; Strabo notes the local river water, like Hierapolis's hot springs, had a mineral-rich, stone-forming quality though it remained drinkable, a detail some modern readers connect to Revelation's "neither cold nor hot" (Revelation 3:15-16), though this too is an inference [bib:strabo-geography; bib:ramsay-seven-churches; scripture:Revelation 3:15-16].

## Materials and colours
Public buildings were of stone and marble, with colonnaded streets under covered walkways. The landscape would show pale travertine-streaked hills near Hierapolis to the north, the darker bulk of Mount Cadmus to the south, and ongoing construction — scaffolding, stacked stone, repaired cracks — consistent with a city still recovering from the AD 60 earthquake.

## Landscape and plants
The Lycus valley supported large flocks of sheep; Strabo describes the region's wool as famous for its softness and glossy raven-black colour, surpassing even Miletus's wool, with nearby Colossae known for wool of a similarly distinctive colour [bib:strabo-geography]. The valley floor would show grazing flocks and cultivated land, with Hierapolis's white terraces visible on the hillside north.

## People and daily life
Laodicea hosted a well-known medical school, associated with physicians Zeuxis and Alexander Philalethes, and was, per Strabo and later writers, the source of a famous eye medicine known as "Phrygian powder" — often connected to Revelation's counsel to buy "eyesalve" (Revelation 3:18), again an inference rather than a statement in the text [bib:strabo-geography; bib:ramsay-seven-churches; scripture:Revelation 3:18]. The city had a substantial Jewish population: a Roman governor's seizure of Jewish Temple contributions in 62 BC recovered a significant quantity of gold from Laodicea alone [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. Colossians 4:15-16 mentions a "Nympha" with a house-church at Laodicea and the exchange of letters with Colossae, implying close contact between the two churches [scripture:Colossians 4:15; scripture:Colossians 4:16]. Daily life would reflect a wealthy trade centre: merchants, wool traders and dyers, students and patients at the medical school, and a mixed Greek, Roman, and Jewish population.

## Key sites to show
- A view across the valley toward Hierapolis's white mineral cliffs — the most distinctive, recognizable feature of the setting, better than isolated ruins for conveying the city.
- A colonnaded street shown partly finished, partly under repair, reflecting the ongoing rebuilding rather than the fully mature later city.
- One of the two theatres, cut into the hillside.
- A wide townscape with Mount Cadmus rising behind the city, conveying scale and setting.

## Not known
The exact extent of rebuilding completed by AD 95 versus still underway is not established: the earthquake was 35 years earlier, but most monumental colonnades, the stadium, and the large nymphaea visible in excavation date to the second century AD or later. Reconstructions should show a city substantially functioning but still visibly recovering, not the fully colonnaded city of a century later, and should avoid later structures such as the Severan Nymphaeum. The precise meaning of "neither cold nor hot" and of the "eyesalve" in Revelation 3 are debated and not stated directly in the text; images should not depict these as literal, explained symbols.
