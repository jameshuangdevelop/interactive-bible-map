# Laodicea — research brief for AI-generated reconstructions

## Setting and layout
Laodicea on the Lycus stood in the Lycus valley, near modern Denizli [bib:strabo-geography; bib:ramsay-seven-churches; dare:21162; wikidata:Q849709]. Strabo says Mount Cadmus lies above the city and that the Lycus flows from it, running underground for most of its course — a sign, he says, that the country is "full of holes and subject to earthquakes" [bib:strabo-geography]. About six miles to the north, in full view across the valley, stood Hierapolis, whose hot springs deposit stone — a landmark still visible today and a defining feature of the view from Laodicea [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; bib:strabo-geography]. Colossae lay about eleven miles to the east, up the valley [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; scripture:Colossians 4:13]. Laodicea lay on the great road running east from Ephesus, at a junction of important routes, which made it a wealthy trade city [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; bib:pau-laodikeia-kazisi].

## What stood then
The Seleucid king Antiochus II founded Laodicea in the mid-third century BC and named it after his wife Laodice; earthquakes struck it under Augustus and again in AD 47, and there was much building under Augustus [bib:pau-laodikeia-kazisi]. Tacitus records that in AD 60, under Nero, "Laodicea, one of the famous Asiatic cities, was laid in ruins by an earthquake, but recovered by its own resources, without assistance from ourselves" [bib:tacitus-annals]; the excavators add that the other cities of the Lycus valley were rebuilt with imperial help [bib:pau-laodikeia-kazisi]. This self-funded rebuilding is often connected by commentators to Revelation's rebuke of the Laodicean church for saying "I am rich ... and have need of nothing" (Revelation 3:17), though Revelation does not state the connection [scripture:Revelation 3:17; bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. By AD 95 much of the city had been rebuilt. A stadium about 285 m long, with marble seating for some 20,000 to 25,000 people, was built by a citizen, Nicostratus, and dedicated to the emperor Titus in AD 79; and in AD 84–85, under Domitian, the city gates (the Ephesus, Syria, Hierapolis and Aphrodisias gates) and the main streets were built with Doric façades [bib:pau-laodikeia-kazisi]. Building went on into the second century: Hadrian visited in AD 135, the South Baths beside the stadium were dedicated to him and his wife Sabina, and most of the remains visible today bear the marks of second-century building, including fountains (nymphaea) named after Trajan, Septimius Severus and Caracalla [bib:pau-laodikeia-kazisi]. Pamukkale University's excavations have uncovered colonnaded streets, two theatres, the stadium, baths, a council house (bouleuterion), temples and agoras [bib:pau-laodikeia-kazisi]. The city's water came by an underground aqueduct from springs about six miles to the south [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]; Strabo notes that the rivers at Laodicea, though drinkable, turn to stone like the springs of Hierapolis, a detail some modern readers connect to Revelation's "neither cold nor hot" (Revelation 3:15-16), though this too is an inference [bib:strabo-geography; bib:ramsay-seven-churches; scripture:Revelation 3:15-16].

## Materials and colours
The stadium's seating was of marble, and the main streets had porticoes on both sides with shop entrances behind them [bib:pau-laodikeia-kazisi]. The sources read for this brief say little else about first-century materials; stone buildings with tiled roofs are general guidance. A wide view would show Hierapolis across the valley to the north, Mount Cadmus above the city, and some building work still under way, since the city kept building into the second century [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; bib:strabo-geography; bib:pau-laodikeia-kazisi].

## Landscape and plants
The Lycus valley supported large flocks of sheep; Strabo describes the region's wool as famous for its softness and raven-black colour, surpassing even Miletus's wool, with nearby Colossae known for wool of a colour named after it [bib:strabo-geography]. The valley floor would show grazing flocks and cultivated land, with Hierapolis visible across the valley to the north [bib:ramsay-seven-churches].

## People and daily life
Strabo records that in his own time a great Herophilean school of medicine was founded by Zeuxis and continued by Alexander Philalethes at the temple of Mên Carus between Laodicea and Carura; Ramsay thinks the school had its seat at Laodicea [bib:strabo-geography; bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. Ramsay also argues that the "Phrygian powder" for the eyes mentioned by Aristotle and Galen came into use through Laodicea, and connects it to Revelation's counsel to buy "eye salve" (Revelation 3:18) — again an inference rather than a statement in the text [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; scripture:Revelation 3:18]. The city had a substantial Jewish population: in 62 BC the Roman governor of Asia seized over twenty pounds of gold collected at Laodicea for the Temple in Jerusalem [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. Colossians 4:15-16 sends greetings to the brothers in Laodicea and to Nymphas and the assembly in his house, and asks for letters to be exchanged with Laodicea, implying close contact between the two churches [scripture:Colossians 4:15; scripture:Colossians 4:16]. Daily life would reflect a wealthy trade centre: merchants, wool traders, physicians and their patients, and a mixed Greek, Roman and Jewish population [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; bib:strabo-geography].

## Key sites to show
- A view across the valley toward Hierapolis's white mineral cliffs — the most distinctive, recognizable feature of the setting, better than isolated ruins for conveying the city.
- A main street with the plain Doric porticoes built under Domitian (AD 84–85), not the later marble embellishments.
- One of the two theatres, cut into the hillside.
- The stadium, dedicated in AD 79, or a wide townscape with Mount Cadmus rising above the city, conveying scale and setting.

## Not known
How much of the city stood finished in AD 95, and what the streets and gates of AD 84–85 looked like in detail, is not known; most of the remains visible today are second-century or later [bib:pau-laodikeia-kazisi]. Reconstructions should show a functioning, largely rebuilt city with its stadium and Domitianic gates and streets, but not the later nymphaea (of Trajan, Septimius Severus and Caracalla), the South Baths or second-century marble embellishment. The first-century appearance of the two theatres is not described in the sources read for this brief. The precise meaning of "neither cold nor hot" and of the "eye salve" in Revelation 3 is debated and not stated directly in the text; images should not depict these as literal, explained symbols.
