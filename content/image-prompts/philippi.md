# Philippi — research brief for AI-generated reconstructions

Period shown: about AD 49–50, Paul's first visit (Acts 16:12-40), when Philippi was a young Roman colony about eight decades removed from the famous 42 BC battle fought on its plain.

## Setting and layout

Philippi lay inland on a fertile, partly marshy plain of eastern Macedonia, on the Via Egnatia, bounded by mountains to the east and north and by Mt Pangaeus to the west, linked to its port Neapolis nine miles south over the pass of Mt Symbolum [bib:isbe-philippi; pleiades:501482; wikidata:Q379652].

## What stood then

Refounded by Philip II of Macedon in 358 BC to work the district's gold mines, the settlement had sunk, per Strabo (as summarized in the Encyclopedia), to a "small settlement" by Julius Caesar's day, before the decisive battle of 42 BC — in which Octavian and Antony defeated Brutus and Cassius on this plain — led to its refounding as the Roman colony Colonia Iulia Philippensis, reinforced after Actium (31 BC) as Colonia Augusta Iulia (Victrix) Philippensium, with the tax-exempting ius Italicum [bib:isbe-philippi]. By Paul's visit it was, as Acts says, "a city of Macedonia, the foremost of the district, a Roman colony" (Acts 16:12), with a forum, magistrates attended by lictors, and a prison (Acts 16:19-24, 16:35-38) [scripture:Acts 16:12; scripture:Acts 16:19-24; bib:isbe-philippi]. Much of what visitors see at the site today is later than Paul: the forum as excavated is of the second half of the 2nd century AD, the theatre (probably built under Philip II) was rebuilt extensively in the 2nd and 3rd centuries AD, and the Octagon church dates from the late 4th century onward [bib:odysseus-philippi]. The 3.5-km city walls, which climb to a fortified acropolis on the hilltop, go back to Philip II [bib:odysseus-philippi]. ISBE notes that "practically nothing" is known of the city's history in the three centuries after Philip II [bib:isbe-philippi].

## Materials and colours

ISBE applies to Philippi Aulus Gellius's description of Roman colonies as "miniatures and pictures" of the Roman people [bib:isbe-philippi]. The sources used here do not describe the colony's building materials or colours, so buildings should stay generic.

## Landscape and plants

The plain is still partly marsh today as in antiquity, crossed by the river the Greeks called the Gangites or Angites, where Paul and his companions went on the Sabbath to find a place of prayer by the riverbank (Acts 16:13) [bib:isbe-philippi; scripture:Acts 16:13].

## People and daily life

ISBE stresses "the very small part played by Jews and Judaism at Philippi": there was no synagogue, the resident Jews were few, and the worshippers who met for prayer on the riverbank were mostly or wholly women, some of them proselytes [bib:isbe-philippi; scripture:Acts 16:13]. Among them was Lydia, a dealer in purple cloth from Thyatira in Asia Minor (Acts 16:14) [scripture:Acts 16:14].
