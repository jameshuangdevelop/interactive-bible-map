# Philadelphia — research brief for AI-generated reconstructions

## Setting and layout
Philadelphia stood in the valley of the Cogamis river, a tributary of the Hermus, at the site of modern Alaşehir [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. It was founded by the Pergamene king Attalus II (reigned 159–138 BC), who earned the epithet "Philadelphus" ("brother-loving") for his loyalty to his brother Eumenes II, and the city was named for that virtue [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. Unlike Sardis or Pergamon, Philadelphia had no great natural acropolis; it stood at the edge of a volcanic plateau the ancients called the Katakekaumene, the "burnt land," a region of extinct volcanoes, black lava flows, and ash-covered ground, known in antiquity chiefly for the excellent vineyards this volcanic soil produced [bib:strabo-geography; bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. The city guarded one of the two main routes from the Hermus and Lydian region eastward into inland Phrygia.

## What stood then
Philadelphia's defining feature by the second half of the first century AD was its long history of earthquakes. Strabo, writing in the 20s AD, calls it a city "ever subject to earthquakes," describing walls and houses with cracks appearing continually, in different parts of the city at different times, so that comparatively few people lived within the walls, most instead farming the surrounding fertile countryside [bib:strabo-geography]. The city was one of the twelve cities struck by the great earthquake of AD 17 that devastated the region; Tacitus lists "the Philadelphenes" among the cities granted tax relief by the emperor Tiberius [bib:tacitus-annals]. Strabo, writing a few years after that disaster, describes a continuing state of alarm at Philadelphia, with many residents still living in huts in the open countryside outside the walls rather than risk the recurring aftershocks, and those who remained within the city reinforcing their walls and houses against further shocks [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. By AD 95, some eighty years later, the worst of this aftershock period had presumably long passed and the city would have been substantially rebuilt, but its reputation for instability and its relatively modest, unfortified-feeling built environment compared to Sardis or Pergamon likely persisted. The grand Byzantine basilica of St. John whose brick piers still dominate the site today is a much later, fifth-to-sixth-century building and must not appear in a first-century reconstruction.

## Materials and colours
Given the volcanic setting, building stone in the region included dark, porous volcanic rock alongside ordinary limestone; ash-covered ground and black lava outcrops mark the surrounding Katakekaumene plain. Because of the earthquake risk, construction may have favoured lower, more modest buildings reinforced against shocks, rather than tall or heavily ornamented structures, though this is an inference rather than a directly attested detail.

## Landscape and plants
The countryside around Philadelphia was given over to vineyards on the volcanic soil of the Katakekaumene, which Strabo says produced wine "inferior to none of the notable wines" [bib:strabo-geography]. The landscape would show dark, ash-grey volcanic ground and rock alongside cultivated vine terraces, with the Cogamis valley providing fertile farmland.

## People and daily life
Revelation's letter to Philadelphia (Revelation 3:7-13) gives the church no rebuke and promises "an open door" and permanence as "a pillar in the temple of my God" who "shall go out thence no more" (Revelation 3:7-8, 12) [scripture:Revelation 3:7; scripture:Revelation 3:12]. Ramsay connects this promise of permanence directly to the city's lived experience of earthquakes, which had repeatedly driven residents out of their homes into the open countryside — a congregation promised they would never again have to flee [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; scripture:Revelation 3:12]. Given the earthquake history, many residents by tradition spent significant time farming outside the walls rather than living permanently within the city, a detail distinct among the seven churches.
