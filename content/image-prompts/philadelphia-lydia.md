# Philadelphia — research brief for AI-generated reconstructions

## Setting and layout
Philadelphia stood in the vale of the Cogamis, a tributary of the Hermus, at the site of modern Alaşehir [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; pleiades:550822; wikidata:Q138280]. It was founded by the Pergamene king Attalus II (reigned 159–138 BC), whose loyalty to his brother Eumenes earned him the epithet "Philadelphus" ("brother-loving"), and the city took its name from him [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. Ramsay describes the site as a broad hill sloping gently up from the vale toward Mount Tmolus, rather than a towering citadel like those of Sardis or Pergamon [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. He places the city on the edge of the Katakekaumene, the "burnt land," a district of extinct volcanoes with rivers of black lava and cinder heaps; Strabo says the plains there are covered with ash, the hills are black, and the land is treeless except for the vines of its notable wine [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; bib:strabo-geography]. The city guarded the road that climbs from the Hermus valley up the Cogamis vale to the Phrygian plateau, one of the two main routes from the west into Phrygia [bib:ramsay-seven-churches].

## What stood then
Philadelphia's defining feature by the second half of the first century AD was its long history of earthquakes. Strabo calls it a city "ever subject to earthquakes," whose house walls cracked incessantly, in different parts of the city at different times, so that few people lived in the city and most lived as farmers in the fertile countryside; the inhabitants planned every structure with the shocks in mind [bib:strabo-geography]. The city was one of the twelve cities struck by the great earthquake of AD 17; Tacitus lists "the Philadelphenes" among the cities granted tax relief by the emperor Tiberius [bib:tacitus-annals]. Ramsay, reading Strabo's account of about AD 20, pictures a continuing panic, with many people living in huts and booths outside the city and those who stayed shoring up their walls and houses, and thinks the habit of going out to the open country had probably not disappeared when Revelation was written [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. By AD 95 the city had presumably been rebuilt, but its name as the city of earthquakes persisted [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. The Church of St John, whose brick piers dominate the site today, is a much later Christian building and must not appear in a first-century reconstruction; the sources read for this brief do not date it.

## Landscape and plants
Most Philadelphians farmed the fertile soil around the city [bib:strabo-geography]. Beyond lay the Katakekaumene, treeless except for the vines that produced a wine "inferior to none of the notable wines" [bib:strabo-geography]. A view could show the city's farmland in the Cogamis vale, Mount Tmolus behind the city, and the dark volcanic country in the distance [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; bib:strabo-geography].

## People and daily life
Revelation's letter to Philadelphia (Revelation 3:7-13) contains no rebuke; it promises "an open door, which no one can shut" (Revelation 3:8), and that the one who overcomes will be "a pillar in the temple of my God, and he will go out from there no more" (Revelation 3:12) [scripture:Revelation 3:7-13; scripture:Revelation 3:8; scripture:Revelation 3:12]. Ramsay connects this promise of permanence to the city's experience of earthquakes, which had repeatedly driven residents out of their homes into the open country [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; scripture:Revelation 3:12]. Strabo says that few people lived in the city and most spent their lives as farmers in the country [bib:strabo-geography].
