# Sardis — research brief for AI-generated reconstructions

## Setting and layout
Sardis stood at the foot of Mount Tmolus (modern Boz Dağ), on the edge of the fertile Hermus river plain, at the site of modern Sart [bib:strabo-geography; bib:sardis-expedition-about]. The city combined a steep, naturally fortified acropolis — called by Polybius "the strongest place in the world" — with a much larger lower city on the plain below [bib:sardis-expedition-about]. The small Pactolus river, which in early times carried the gold dust that Strabo says gave rise to the fame of King Croesus's riches, ran down from Tmolus past the city into the Hermus [bib:strabo-geography]. Above the city, the summit of Tmolus held a lookout with a white marble arcade, "a work of the Persians," with a view over the plains below [bib:strabo-geography]. In the first century AD Sardis was a major city of the Roman province of Asia, rebuilt after a catastrophic earthquake in AD 17 [bib:tacitus-annals; bib:strabo-geography].

## What stood then
In AD 17 an earthquake struck twelve important cities of Asia in a single night; Tacitus records that Sardis suffered the worst damage and received the largest relief — ten million sesterces and a five-year remission of its payments to the treasury — and that a senatorial commissioner was sent to the stricken cities to assess the damage and give relief [bib:tacitus-annals]. Strabo, writing only a few years later, says Sardis "has lost many of its buildings through earthquakes" but that "the forethought of Tiberius" had restored it [bib:strabo-geography]. By AD 95, some eighty years on, Sardis would have substantially recovered under this Tiberian rebuilding, with a functioning civic centre, though the grandest Roman-period monuments visitors see today came later still [bib:sardis-expedition-bath-gymnasium]. The Temple of Artemis, one of the largest temples in the world, had its main building (cella) built in the third century BC, and its outer colonnade was planned but not begun in that period [bib:sardis-expedition-artemis-temple]. In Roman times (the first or second century AD) the cella was divided in two, probably for the imperial cult, and probably then the outer colonnade was begun at the east end; those columns were erected but never finished, only foundations were laid on the long sides, and on the west no columns were begun [bib:sardis-expedition-artemis-temple]. By AD 95, therefore, the temple was either a large cella without its outer colonnade or one with work just beginning at its east end. The famous Bath-Gymnasium complex with its "Marble Court" was probably completed in the late second or early third century AD (the Marble Court is dedicated to Caracalla, Geta and Julia Domna), and the synagogue — the largest known from the ancient world — was made later from one of its halls; neither should appear in a reconstruction of first-century Sardis [bib:sardis-expedition-bath-gymnasium; bib:sardis-expedition-about]. The excavators note that although Sardis is one of the seven churches of Revelation, no church buildings dating to this era have been found [bib:sardis-expedition-about].

## Materials and colours
The acropolis is a steep, crumbling rock outcrop, its soft rock weathering away [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. The sources read for this brief do not describe the lower city's first-century building materials; stone footings, fired brick, marble for temples and tiled roofs are general guidance, not sourced details. The Temple of Artemis would show its finished cella, with at most bare column bases or foundations where the colonnade was planned [bib:sardis-expedition-artemis-temple].

## Landscape and plants
Strabo calls the plains below Sardis the best of all plains and Mount Tmolus above it "a blest mountain" [bib:strabo-geography]. Vineyards and orchards on its lower slopes are a general inference, not a sourced detail; the Pactolus runs down from the mountain to the plain [bib:strabo-geography].

## People and daily life
Revelation's letter to Sardis rebukes a church with "a reputation of being alive" but "dead" (Revelation 3:1), promising that the faithful few "will walk with me in white" (Revelation 3:4) [scripture:Revelation 3:1; scripture:Revelation 3:4]. Ramsay connects the warning "I will come as a thief" (Revelation 3:3) to local history: the citadel, trusted as impregnable, was taken by Cyrus in the sixth century BC and again by Antiochus III about three centuries later, both times by climbers on a side left unguarded; Ramsay argues that Cyrus's attack must have been made by night [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; scripture:Revelation 3:3]. Daily life in this rebuilt provincial city would show citizens, officials, farmers working the Hermus plain, and traders on the major route linking the Aegean with inland Anatolia [bib:sardis-expedition-about].

## Key sites to show
- A wide view of the steep acropolis above the Hermus plain, with Mount Tmolus behind — the best "overall feel" shot.
- The Temple of Artemis shown as a large, unfinished building: its complete cella with no outer colonnade, or with work just beginning at the east end, not the fuller Roman colonnade seen in later-period reconstructions.
- The Pactolus stream valley below the acropolis.

## Not known
The precise look of the Tiberian rebuilding after AD 17 is not well documented in detail; the general fact of recovery, but not specific restored buildings, is known. The Bath-Gymnasium complex, its Marble Court, and the synagogue are all later than our period and must not appear. Any image of the Temple of Artemis must show it incomplete, not with the full exterior colonnade sometimes seen in artists' reconstructions of its final (still unfinished) Roman-period state.
