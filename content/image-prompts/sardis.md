# Sardis — research brief for AI-generated reconstructions

## Setting and layout
Sardis stood at the foot of Mount Tmolus (modern Boz Dağ), on the edge of the fertile Hermus river plain, at the site of modern Sart [bib:strabo-geography; bib:sardis-expedition-about]. The city combined a steep, naturally fortified acropolis — called by Polybius "the strongest place in the world" — with a much larger lower city on the plain below [bib:sardis-expedition-about]. The small Pactolus river, which earlier carried gold dust that Strabo says gave rise to the legendary wealth of King Croesus, ran down from Tmolus through the city [bib:strabo-geography]. Above the city, the summit of Tmolus held a lookout with a white marble arcade, a Persian-period work, with a view over the plains below [bib:strabo-geography]. By the second half of the first century AD, Sardis was a major city of the Roman province of Asia, still recovering from a catastrophic earthquake in AD 17.

## What stood then
In AD 17 an earthquake destroyed or badly damaged twelve cities of the Lydian region in a single night; Tacitus records that Sardis suffered the worst damage and received the largest relief package from Tiberius — ten million sesterces and a five-year tax remission — with a senatorial commissioner sent to oversee reconstruction [bib:tacitus-annals]. Strabo, writing only a few years later, confirms Sardis had "lost many of its buildings through earthquakes" but that "the forethought of Tiberius...has...restored" the city [bib:strabo-geography]. By AD 95, some eighty years on, Sardis would have substantially recovered under this Tiberian rebuilding, with a functioning civic centre, though the grandest Roman-period monuments visitors see today came later still. The Temple of Artemis, one of the largest temples in Asia Minor, had its inner chamber (cella) built in the Hellenistic period (third century BC); its grand outer colonnade of marble columns was only begun in the first or second century AD and never finished — by AD 95 the temple would have appeared large but visibly incomplete, its cella finished but its colonnade only beginning to rise or not yet begun on most sides [bib:sardis-expedition-artemis-temple]. The famous Bath-Gymnasium complex with its "Marble Court," and the synagogue later built into one of its halls — the largest known synagogue from the ancient world — both date from the late second or early third century AD at the earliest, and should **not** appear in a reconstruction of first-century Sardis [bib:sardis-expedition-bath-gymnasium]. The excavators note that although Sardis is one of the seven churches of Revelation, no church building from this era has been identified archaeologically [bib:sardis-expedition-about].

## Materials and colours
The acropolis is a steep, pale, crumbling rock outcrop, its instability noted by ancient writers; the lower city's Roman-period buildings used stone footings, fired brick, and marble for temples and colonnades, with tiled roofs. The unfinished Temple of Artemis would show pale marble on its finished cella and column stumps or bare foundations where the colonnade was incomplete.

## Landscape and plants
The Hermus plain around Sardis is fertile agricultural land; Mount Tmolus rises behind the city, described as a "blest mountain." Vineyards and orchards were cultivated on its lower slopes, with the Pactolus stream running down to the plain.

## People and daily life
Revelation's letter to Sardis rebukes a church with "a reputation of being alive" but spiritually "dead" (Revelation 3:1), promising the faithful will "walk with me in white" (Revelation 3:4) [scripture:Revelation 3:1; scripture:Revelation 3:4]. Ramsay connects the warning "I will come as a thief" (Revelation 3:3) to local history: the acropolis was twice taken by surprise night attacks on an unguarded cliff path, by Cyrus the Great in the sixth century BC and Antiochus III in 214 BC [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; scripture:Revelation 3:3]. Daily life in this recovering provincial city would show citizens, officials, farmers working the Hermus plain, and traders on the major route the city commanded.

## Key sites to show
- A wide view of the steep acropolis above the Hermus plain, with Mount Tmolus behind — the best "overall feel" shot.
- The Temple of Artemis shown as a large, partly unfinished building: a complete cella with a colonnade only beginning to rise or largely absent, not the fuller Roman colonnade seen in later-period reconstructions.
- The Pactolus stream valley below the acropolis.

## Not known
The precise look of the Tiberian rebuilding after AD 17 is not well documented in detail; the general fact of recovery, but not specific restored buildings, is known. The Bath-Gymnasium complex, its Marble Court, and the synagogue are all later than our period and must not appear. Any image of the Temple of Artemis must show it incomplete, not with the full exterior colonnade sometimes seen in artists' reconstructions of its final (still unfinished) Roman-period state.
