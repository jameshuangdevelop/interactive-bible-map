# *Golgotha* — research brief for AI-generated reconstructions

## Setting and layout
All four Gospels name the place of the crucifixion Golgotha, "the place of a skull" (Luke calls it "The Skull") [scripture:Matthew 27:33; scripture:Mark 15:22; scripture:Luke 23:33; scripture:John 19:17]; John adds that it "was near the city," and Hebrews that Jesus "suffered outside of the gate" [scripture:John 19:20; scripture:Hebrews 13:12]. For most of this period Jerusalem had only two walls; the outer "third wall" was merely begun, as foundations, by Herod Agrippa I in the early 40s AD and raised to full height only later, so ground that lay outside the city at the crucifixion could later be enclosed within it [bib:josephus-jewish-war]. Taylor defines Golgotha as the area of a disused quarry west of first-century Jerusalem, close to the Gennath Gate and two main roads; the Church of the Holy Sepulchre now stands in this area, inside today's Old City [bib:taylor-golgotha-reconsideration; wikidata:Q187702]. The Garden Tomb, a separate candidate about 600 meters north of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, lies among rock-cut tombs outside today's walls [wikidata:Q1494672; wikidata:Q187702].

## What stood then
At the time of the crucifixion there was no built monument at either candidate site. John says that "in the place where he was crucified there was a garden," with "a new tomb" in it [scripture:John 19:41]. Taylor describes the traditional area as a disused quarry and proposes that the execution and the burial took place at separate spots within it, the tomb about 200 m further north in a more isolated area [bib:taylor-golgotha-reconsideration]. The tomb chamber at the Garden Tomb is dated by most archaeologists many centuries earlier — Taylor's reconsideration of the evidence (1998, pp. 180-203), drawing on Barkay's and Biddle's studies of the tomb's form, places it well before the first century — a difficulty for identifying it with the Gospels' "new" tomb [bib:taylor-golgotha-reconsideration]. The churches and monuments that mark both sites today were all built centuries after this period, beginning under Constantine in the fourth century [wikidata:Q187702].

## Materials and colours
The setting is the same pale cream to golden-white Jerusalem limestone common across the city's quarries — the same stone Josephus describes in the Temple's foundations and cloisters — left in rough, tool-marked faces where cut for building stone, with smoother natural rock where tombs were carved into it [bib:josephus-jewish-war; bib:ritmeyer-temple-mount-quest].

## Landscape and plants
The site combined quarry scars with cultivated ground: a garden adjoining or surrounding the tombs, suited to the olive trees and low scrub typical of this hill country, where terracing for cultivation was standard practice [scripture:John 19:41; bib:isbe-agriculture; bib:rainey-notley-sacred-bridge]. The Gospels say passersby mocked Jesus at the cross, and Taylor places the execution site close to two main roads [scripture:Mark 15:29; bib:taylor-golgotha-reconsideration].

## People and daily life
A Roman execution detail carried out the crucifixion, with a centurion overseeing it [scripture:Mark 15:39]. Women who had followed Jesus in Galilee watched from a distance [scripture:Mark 15:40-41]. Two others condemned to death were crucified alongside him [scripture:Luke 23:33].

## Key sites to show
1. A quarried limestone escarpment with tool marks, kept generic rather than matching either candidate's exact modern appearance.
2. A rock-cut tomb entrance of the rolling-stone type used in this period.
3. A garden setting among the quarry remains, with scattered olive trees.

## Not known
Which candidate site, if either precisely, is the historical Golgotha is not settled, and this project does not choose between them [bib:taylor-golgotha-reconsideration]. The exact course of Jerusalem's wall at the time of the crucifixion is debated, so the precise distance from the city gate is not fixed. The rock formation popularly compared to a skull near the Garden Tomb is a nineteenth-century observation, not an ancient description, and should not be presented as how the site looked or was understood in the first century. Images should show a generic quarry-and-garden setting outside the wall, without later church buildings, and without favoring one candidate's modern landscape over the other.
