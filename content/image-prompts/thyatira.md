# Thyatira — research brief for AI-generated reconstructions

## Setting and layout
Thyatira stood in a long, open valley connecting the Hermus and Caicus river valleys, at the site of modern Akhisar in western Turkey, on the left bank of a stream feeding the Lycus (a tributary of the Hermus, not the larger Lycus of the Laodicea region) [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. Strabo describes it, on the road from Pergamon to Sardis, as "a settlement of the Macedonians," noting that some called it the farthest outpost of the Mysians, which places it on the frontier between Mysia and Lydia [bib:strabo-geography]. It was founded as a military colony — Seleucid in origin, with Macedonian veteran-settlers — guarding this valley route rather than a major natural stronghold, unlike the hill-cities of Sardis and Pergamon [bib:strabo-geography; bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. Because the open valley gave no natural defensive advantage, the city's layout would have been a planned, relatively level grid rather than a hill town.

## What stood then
Almost all of ancient Thyatira lies unexcavated beneath the modern town of Akhisar, so comparatively little is known about its specific Roman-period buildings; this brief's description is necessarily more general than for the other six churches. Inscriptions recovered from the site show an unusually large number of organized trade guilds for a city of its size — wool-workers, linen-workers, makers of outer garments, dyers, leather-workers, tanners, potters, bakers, and bronze-smiths are all attested — reflecting a city whose identity and civic life centred on manufacturing and trade rather than on grand public monuments [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. The city's patron deity, worshipped under the composite title Apollo Tyrimnaios (also called the hero Tyrimnos), combined Greek and Anatolian elements: coins show him as a standing or mounted figure in a cloak, carrying a battle-axe over one shoulder and a laurel branch, an image tied to the city's identity as a garrison foundation [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. Acts 16:14 identifies Lydia, a convert baptized at Philippi, as "a seller of purple, of the city of Thyatira," which fits the city's documented dyeing trade, though the dye she sold was most likely a locally produced colour rather than the shellfish-derived Tyrian purple of the Mediterranean coast, since Thyatira lies well inland [scripture:Acts 16:14; bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. By later coin evidence the city was prosperous, suggesting wealth built on this trade and craft base rather than on grand civic architecture of the kind seen at Pergamon or Sardis.

## Materials and colours
With little standing or excavated architecture to describe, this brief can only generalize: ordinary Roman provincial building in the region used local stone footings with mudbrick or rubble walls, tiled roofs, and plastered, often whitewashed, wall surfaces, with more substantial public buildings (temple, guild halls, colonnaded streets typical of planned Hellenistic-Roman cities) in finer stone. Workshops tied to the dyeing and textile trades would have shown vats, drying racks, and cloth in shades of red (the probable local dye colour) and undyed wool and linen.

## Landscape and plants
The surrounding valley is agricultural, part of the wider Hermus river system, suited to grain, vines, and pasture for sheep, supporting the wool trade the inscriptions attest.

## People and daily life
Revelation's letter to Thyatira (Revelation 2:18-29) addresses a congregation in conflict over a local "prophetess," without the city-specific civic detail found in some other letters [scripture:Revelation 2:18-20]. Given the guild inscriptions, daily life would have revolved heavily around craft workshops — dyers, weavers, potters, bronze-smiths, tanners, and bakers — organized into guilds that likely had their own patron-deity associations and social functions, a backdrop plausibly relevant to the "food sacrificed to idols" controversy the letter addresses [scripture:Revelation 2:20; bib:ramsay-seven-churches].

## Not known
Thyatira is the least-excavated of the seven churches' cities; its Roman-period street plan, major public buildings, and city walls are essentially unknown archaeologically, since the ancient site is largely built over by modern Akhisar. Any reconstruction image must keep the city's specific architecture generic and avoid inventing named monuments, temples, or a city plan not attested by excavation. The look of the city's patron-deity temple and its exact location are also not known and should not be shown in specific detail.
