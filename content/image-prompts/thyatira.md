# Thyatira — research brief for AI-generated reconstructions

## Setting and layout
Thyatira stood in an open vale running north–south between the Hermus and Caicus valleys, near the left bank of the Lycus, one of the chief tributaries of the Hermus (not the Lycus of the Laodicea region), at the site of modern Akhisar in western Turkey [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; dare:21159; wikidata:Q1135603]. Strabo, describing the road from Pergamon to Sardis, calls it "a settlement of the Macedonians," which some call "the farthermost city of the Mysians" [bib:strabo-geography]. Ramsay says Seleucus I planted a colony of Macedonian soldiers there between 300 and 282 BC to hold this road, and describes the site as an open vale between gently sloping hills, with only a very slight rise to serve as a citadel, far less defensible than Sardis or Pergamon [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. A fairly level town, perhaps laid out on a grid, is a reasonable inference from this setting, not an attested plan.

## What stood then
Ancient Thyatira lies under the modern town of Akhisar [dare:21159; wikidata:Q1135603], and the sources read for this brief describe none of its Roman-period buildings, so this brief is necessarily more general than those for the other churches. Ramsay notes that more trade guilds are known at Thyatira than in any other city of Asia: the inscriptions name wool-workers, linen-workers, makers of outer garments, dyers, leather-workers, tanners, potters, bakers, slave-dealers and bronze-smiths [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. The city's god, styled in inscriptions Helios Pythian Tyrimnaean Apollo, appears on coins as a standing figure in a cloak, with a battle-axe over one shoulder and a laurel branch; a closely related hero, Tyrimnos, rides on horseback with a battle-axe, an image Ramsay ties to the city's origin as a garrison [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. Acts identifies Lydia, whom Paul baptised at Philippi, as "a seller of purple, of the city of Thyatira" [scripture:Acts 16:14-15]. Ramsay argues that her "purple" was a local dye rather than the shellfish purple of the coast, most likely the bright "Turkey red" made from madder root, which grows abundantly in the region [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]. Ramsay also notes that the city's coinage was struck on a great scale in the third century, a sign of prosperity built on this trade and craft base [bib:ramsay-seven-churches].

## Materials and colours
With little standing or excavated architecture to describe, this brief can only generalize: ordinary Roman provincial building in the region used local stone footings with mudbrick or rubble walls, tiled roofs, and plastered, often whitewashed, wall surfaces, with more substantial public buildings (temple, guild halls, colonnaded streets typical of planned Hellenistic-Roman cities) in finer stone. Workshops tied to the dyeing and textile trades would have shown vats, drying racks, and cloth in shades of red (the probable local dye colour, from madder root [bib:ramsay-seven-churches]) and undyed wool and linen.

## Landscape and plants
The sources read for this brief do not describe the vale's farming. Grain, vines and sheep pasture, which would fit the attested wool-workers, are a general inference, not a sourced detail.

## People and daily life
Revelation's letter to Thyatira (Revelation 2:18-29) rebukes the congregation for tolerating a woman "who calls herself a prophetess" and who teaches people to eat "things sacrificed to idols," without the city-specific civic detail found in some other letters [scripture:Revelation 2:18-20]. Given the guild inscriptions, daily life would have revolved heavily around craft workshops — dyers, weavers, potters, bronze-smiths, tanners and bakers — and Ramsay discusses the guilds' common meals and religious ties as the background to that controversy [bib:ramsay-seven-churches; scripture:Revelation 2:20].

- A craft/guild quarter scene suggested by the trade inscriptions (dyeing, weaving, bronze-working), kept generic rather than tied to a specific excavated building.

## Not known
The sources read for this brief describe none of Roman Thyatira's street plan, public buildings or walls, since the ancient city lies under modern Akhisar. Any reconstruction image must keep the city's specific architecture generic and avoid inventing named monuments, temples, or a city plan not attested by excavation. The look of the city's patron-deity temple and its exact location are also not known and should not be shown in specific detail.
