# *Bethlehem* — research brief for AI-generated reconstructions

## Setting and layout
Bethlehem sits in the Judean hill country about 8 km south of Jerusalem, on a ridge among terraced hills at roughly 775 meters elevation [wikidata:Q5776; dare:28331]. In this period it was a small town rather than a major city, identified in Matthew and Luke as Jesus' birthplace and already known in the Old Testament as David's home town [scripture:Matthew 2:1; scripture:Luke 2:4]. Herod the Great's hilltop palace-fortress of Herodium stood on a mountain a few kilometers to the southeast (Wikidata's point for Herodium sits about 5 km from Bethlehem's own), a visible reminder of Herodian building even from this modest town [wikidata:Q913099; wikidata:Q5776; bib:rainey-notley-sacred-bridge].

## What stood then
No grand public buildings are attested at Bethlehem in this period; it was a rural hill-country town of domestic houses [bib:murphy-oconnor-holy-land-guide]. Tradition places Jesus' birth in a cave — UNESCO notes that one particular cave, over which the first church was built, has been venerated as the birthplace since at least the second century — and Luke mentions a feeding trough [scripture:Luke 2:7; bib:unesco-church-of-nativity]. Caves were the region's oldest form of dwelling, and as stone-built houses developed alongside them, houses in this hill country commonly incorporated a cave or lower story for livestock [bib:isbe-house; bib:murphy-oconnor-holy-land-guide]. The Church of the Nativity, first completed around AD 339 and rebuilt under Justinian in the mid-sixth century, did not yet exist in this period and should not appear in a reconstruction [bib:unesco-church-of-nativity].

## Materials and colours
Houses were built of local Judean limestone, pale cream to grey, with flat roofs of timber beams, brushwood and packed earth or plaster — ISBE's account of the region's house-building describes exactly this progression from cave to simple stone hut "covered with a roof of brushwood and mud" — typical of the hill country [bib:isbe-house; bib:rainey-notley-sacred-bridge]. Caves and rock-cut spaces beneath or beside houses were commonly incorporated into the building rather than excavated separately [bib:isbe-house; bib:murphy-oconnor-holy-land-guide].

## Landscape and plants
The surrounding slopes were terraced for olives, grapevines and grain: terracing hillsides for cultivation was standard practice in this region, and old terraces of this kind still survive [bib:isbe-agriculture; bib:rainey-notley-sacred-bridge]. Shepherds grazed flocks on nearby hillsides, the setting for the Gospel's shepherds who were "in the same country staying in the field, and keeping watch by night over their flock" [scripture:Luke 2:8].

## People and daily life
Bethlehem's population in this period were rural Judean villagers and shepherds, dressed in simple wool tunics and cloaks [bib:freedman-anchor-bible-dictionary]. The Gospel narratives describe shepherds, a census decreed by Caesar Augustus bringing travelers to the town, and later wise men arriving from the east [scripture:Luke 2:1-4; scripture:Luke 2:8; scripture:Matthew 2:1].

## Not known
The precise layout and boundary of first-century Bethlehem are not well recovered archaeologically, since the town has been continuously inhabited and built over [bib:murphy-oconnor-holy-land-guide]. No first-century structure survives at the traditional birth site beneath the later church to show directly; images should depict a generic cave-and-house dwelling rather than the present-day church interior.
